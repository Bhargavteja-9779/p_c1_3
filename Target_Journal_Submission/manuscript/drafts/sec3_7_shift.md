### 3.7 Batch shift, laboratory shift and unseen protocols

{{FIG:Fig10_distribution_shift|Screening under distribution shift (E5, {{CFG.n_secondary}} splits per case, elastic net). Batch shift: models trained and calibrated on MATR batches b1 and b2 and applied to batch b3 or to the CLO batch b4, either with the source calibration cells or after recalibration with 20 or 30 cells of the target batch cycled to $L^\ast$ only. Laboratory shift: capacity-trajectory features only, MATR/CLO model or HUST model, calibrated on HUST cells and tested on other HUST cells ($L^\ast$ = {{D.h.Lstar}}). Points: mean error rates with 95% confidence intervals.}}

The guarantee rests on exchangeability between calibration and screened cells, and the shift experiments show what happens without it ({{FIGREF:Fig10_distribution_shift}}; {{STABREF:tables/T8_shift.csv}}). The MATR batches differ strongly: with $L^\ast$ = 750 cycles, all 43 cells of b2 but only 3 of the 40 cells of b3 and 18 of the 45 CLO cells are bad, because the batches were cycled with different protocol sets. A model trained and calibrated on b1 and b2 and applied to b3 without recalibration rejected about half of the good b3 cells (false-reject rate {{E5.b3_source.proposed.frr}}) and passed {{E5.b3_source.proposed.fpr}} of the bad ones; on b4 the false-reject rate was {{E5.b4_source.proposed.frr}}. The parametric and naive rules failed similarly, and the single-checkpoint rule exchanged a low false-reject rate for a false-pass rate of {{E5.b4_source.single_k100.fpr}} on b4. Recalibrating with 20 cells of the target batch restored both error targets (b3: {{E5.b3_target20.proposed.fpr}} and {{E5.b3_target20.proposed.frr}}; b4: {{E5.b4_target20.proposed.fpr}} and {{E5.b4_target20.proposed.frr}}), but at a price: with so few target cells, and only one or two bad cells among them for b3, the order statistic needed to pass a cell often did not exist, so most cells were sent to full observation (cycling saved {{E5.b3_target20.proposed.sav}}% on b3 and {{E5.b4_target30.proposed.sav}}% on b4 with 30 cells). The point-prediction rule happened to work on b3 but passed {{E5.b4_source.point_k100.fpr}} of the bad b4 cells, which illustrates that an uncalibrated rule can look safe on one batch and fail on the next.

Across laboratories, a model trained on MATR/CLO capacity trajectories and recalibrated on HUST cells was valid on the remaining HUST cells (false-pass {{E5.xlab_source.proposed.fpr}}, false-reject {{E5.xlab_source.proposed.frr}}), although it saved only {{E5.xlab_source.proposed.sav}}% of the cycling against {{E5.xlab_target.proposed.sav}}% for a model trained on HUST cells. Recalibration on the target population therefore transfers validity even when the base model comes from another laboratory; efficiency still depends on how well the model fits the new cells.

When whole charging protocols were held out, so that no protocol of a test cell was seen in training or calibration (E9, {{E9.n_groups}} protocol groups of MATR/CLO), the trajectory rule had false-pass and false-reject rates of {{E9.mc.proposed.fpr}} and {{E9.mc.proposed.frr}} and saved {{E9.mc.proposed.sav}}% of the cycling. Exchangeability then holds at the level of protocols rather than cells, so the guarantee applies to new protocols drawn from the same design space, not to protocols outside it.
