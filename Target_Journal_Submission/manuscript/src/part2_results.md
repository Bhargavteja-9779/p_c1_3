The trajectory-calibrated rule met both error targets on both data sets ({{FIGREF:Fig4_validity_and_efficiency}}, {{TABREF:tables/T2_main_results.csv}}). On MATR/CLO its mean false-pass rate was {{E1.mc.proposed.fpr}} (95% CI {{E1.mc.proposed.fpr_lo}}–{{E1.mc.proposed.fpr_hi}}) and its false-reject rate {{E1.mc.proposed.frr}} ({{E1.mc.proposed.frr_lo}}–{{E1.mc.proposed.frr_hi}}); on HUST they were {{E1.h.proposed.fpr}} ({{E1.h.proposed.fpr_lo}}–{{E1.h.proposed.fpr_hi}}) and {{E1.h.proposed.frr}} ({{E1.h.proposed.frr_lo}}–{{E1.h.proposed.frr_hi}}). With the realised calibration-set sizes, the rank rule attains an exact level of {{E1.mc.lvl_bad}} (false pass) and {{E1.mc.lvl_good}} (false reject) on MATR/CLO and {{E1.h.lvl_bad}} and {{E1.h.lvl_good}} on HUST rather than 0.10, because the order statistic moves in steps of $1/(n_0+1)$; for continuous scores the bound of Proposition 1 is otherwise tight, so the maximum over checkpoints is not conservative. The realised rates fall slightly below these levels because some bad cells are rejected (and some good cells passed) before their trajectory maximum would have crossed the opposite threshold. As the theory predicts, the bound holds on average rather than in every split: the realised false-pass rate exceeded 0.10 in {{E1.mc.proposed.fpr_above}}% (MATR/CLO) and {{E1.h.proposed.fpr_above}}% (HUST) of the splits, with about {{E1.mc.proposed.ncal_bad}} and {{E1.h.proposed.ncal_bad}} bad cells, respectively, in the test sets' companion calibration sets. The single-checkpoint conformal rule and the proposed rule without normalisation were also valid, as Proposition 1 (with $M=1$, respectively without normalisation) implies. The lifetime-interval rule had even lower class-conditional error rates, but this is an empirical finding: its marginal coverage guarantee bounds only the joint probability of a wrong decision, not the error rate within each class.

The rules without a valid calibration behaved very differently. Using individually valid per-checkpoint thresholds sequentially (naive sequential) inflated the false-pass rate to {{E1.mc.naive.fpr}} on MATR/CLO and {{E1.h.naive.fpr}} on HUST, and the false-reject rate to {{E1.mc.naive.frr}} and {{E1.h.naive.frr}} — up to {{RATIO.naive_max}} times the nominal level. The Severson-style point rule at cycle 100 happened to stay below 0.10 on MATR/CLO ({{E1.mc.point_k100.fpr}} and {{E1.mc.point_k100.frr}}) but not on HUST ({{E1.h.point_k100.fpr}} and {{E1.h.point_k100.frr}}). The parametric rule with Gaussian thresholds on the same normalised scores was conservative on MATR/CLO ({{E1.mc.gauss.fpr}}, {{E1.mc.gauss.frr}}) and violated the false-reject target on HUST ({{E1.h.gauss.frr}}, upper confidence limit {{E1.h.gauss.frr_hi}}). Bonferroni calibration never passed or rejected a cell, because with about {{E1.mc.bonf.ncal_bad}} bad and {{E1.mc.bonf.ncal_good}} good calibration cells the required order statistic at level $\alpha/M$ does not exist.

### 3.4 Other base predictors

{{TABLE:tables/T4_base_models.csv|Validity and efficiency of the screening rules with different base predictors (E2; elastic net from E1). FPR: false-pass rate among bad test cells; FRR: false-reject rate among good test cells; saved: fraction of cycling saved relative to observing every test cell to $\min(T,L^\ast)$. For the Gaussian process the parametric rule is the Bayesian posterior-probability rule. Number of splits: {{CFG.n_main}} (elastic net, logistic), {{CFG.n_gbr}} (GBR, RF, GP) and {{CFG.n_cnn}} (CNN).|dataset:Data set:s,model:Model:s,method:Rule:s,fpr_mean:FPR:.3f,frr_mean:FRR:.3f,early_rate_mean:Early:pct1,time_saving_mean:Saved:pct1,n_splits:Splits:int}}

The guarantee of Proposition 1 does not depend on the base predictor, and the experiments agree ({{TABREF:tables/T4_base_models.csv}}). With logistic regression, gradient-boosted trees, random forests, a Gaussian process and a convolutional network, the proposed rule kept both mean error rates at or below the nominal 0.10 in every case except the CNN on HUST, whose false-reject rate of {{E2.h.cnn.proposed.frr}} (upper 95% limit {{E2.h.cnn.proposed.frr_hi}}) rests on only {{CFG.n_cnn}} splits and is compatible with the target within Monte-Carlo error. The naive sequential rule exceeded the targets with every model. The choice of model mattered for efficiency, not for validity: on MATR/CLO the trajectory rule saved {{E2.mc.gbr.proposed.sav}}% (GBR), {{E2.mc.rf.proposed.sav}}% (RF), {{E2.mc.gp.proposed.sav}}% (GP) and {{E2.mc.cnn.proposed.sav}}% (CNN) of the cycling, more than the single-checkpoint rule with the same model ({{E2.mc.gbr.single_k100.sav}}%, {{E2.mc.rf.single_k100.sav}}%, {{E2.mc.gp.single_k100.sav}}% and {{E2.mc.cnn.single_k100.sav}}%), so on MATR/CLO a fixed checkpoint at cycle 100 was as efficient as the sequential rule only with the elastic net and more efficient only with logistic regression ({{E2.mc.logit.single_k100.sav}}% against {{E2.mc.logit.proposed.sav}}%). On HUST the trajectory rule saved more than the single-checkpoint rule with every model.

Model-based uncertainty did not provide control either. The Bayesian rule of the Gaussian process, which passes a cell when its posterior probability of reaching $L^\ast$ exceeds $1-\alpha$, had false-pass and false-reject rates of {{E2.h.gp.gauss.fpr}} and {{E2.h.gp.gauss.frr}} on HUST and a false-reject rate of {{E2.mc.gp.gauss.frr}} on MATR/CLO: the posterior was over-confident for cells unlike the training cells. Logistic regression, trained only on the class of each cell and therefore on training cells cycled to $\min(T,L^\ast)$ rather than to end of life, gave valid decisions but saved less cycling ({{E2.mc.logit.proposed.sav}}% on MATR/CLO and {{E2.h.logit.proposed.sav}}% on HUST) than the elastic net trained on full lifetimes ({{E1.mc.proposed.sav}}% and {{E1.h.proposed.sav}}%). A laboratory that has no full-lifetime history can therefore start screening with a truncated training campaign, at the cost of less efficient decisions.

### 3.5 How many checkpoints?

{{FIG:Fig7_ablation_checkpoint_grid|Checkpoint-grid ablation (E3, {{CFG.n_secondary}} splits per data set, elastic net). Cycling saved by the trajectory-calibrated rule for different checkpoint grids, together with the false-pass and false-reject rates; the naive sequential rule is shown for reference. With a single checkpoint all rules coincide.}}

The number of looks trades flexibility against conservativeness ({{FIGREF:Fig7_ablation_checkpoint_grid}}; {{STABREF:tables/T6_ablation_grid.csv}}). Every grid kept both error rates of the trajectory rule below 0.10, whereas the naive sequential rule became more invalid as looks were added: on HUST its false-pass rate rose from {{E3.h.single_100.naive.fpr}} with one checkpoint to {{E3.h.pair_100_1000.naive.fpr}} with two, {{E3.h.sparse_100_300_600_1000.naive.fpr}} with four and {{E3.h.all17.naive.fpr}} with all 17. For efficiency, the dense grids of the main experiment were not optimal. On MATR/CLO a sparse grid of cycles 50, 100, 200 and 300 saved {{E3.mc.sparse_50_100_200_300.proposed.sav}}% of the cycling, against {{E3.mc.all16.proposed.sav}}% with all 16 checkpoints and {{E3.mc.single_50.proposed.sav}}% with the best single checkpoint (cycle 50). On HUST the sparse grid (cycles 100, 300, 600, 1000) saved {{E3.h.sparse_100_300_600_1000.proposed.sav}}% against {{E3.h.all17.proposed.sav}}% with all 17 checkpoints, but a single checkpoint at cycle 300 saved more ({{E3.h.single_300.proposed.sav}}%). Each additional checkpoint raises the calibrated thresholds, because the maximum of more scores is larger; checkpoints that add little information therefore cost efficiency. The best single checkpoint, however, differed between the data sets (cycle 50 for MATR/CLO, cycle 300 for HUST; cycle 100 was a poor choice for HUST, {{E3.h.single_100.proposed.sav}}%), and it cannot be chosen on the calibration cells without voiding the guarantee. It can, however, be chosen on the training cells, which leaves calibration and test cells exchangeable. A sparse grid of three to five checkpoints spread over the period in which the early-cycle features become informative (Section 3.9) was the most robust choice across both data sets; we report the dense grids in the main experiment because they were fixed before the experiments were run.

**Choosing the grid on training cells (E12).** To compare the rules fairly, we chose the grid in every split from the candidates of E3 using the training cells only: out-of-fold scores of the training cells (five-fold cross-fitting) were used to calibrate each candidate grid and to measure its cycling saving on the same cells and evaluated the choice on the untouched calibration and test cells (same splits as E3). Restricted to multi-checkpoint grids, the selection picked the sparse grid most often ({{SEL.mc.seq_sel.top_grid}} in {{SEL.mc.seq_sel.top_share}}% of the MATR/CLO splits, {{SEL.h.seq_sel.top_grid}} in {{SEL.h.seq_sel.top_share}}% on HUST) and saved {{SEL.mc.seq_sel.sav}}% and {{SEL.h.seq_sel.sav}}% of the cycling with error rates of {{SEL.mc.seq_sel.fpr}}/{{SEL.mc.seq_sel.frr}} and {{SEL.h.seq_sel.fpr}}/{{SEL.h.seq_sel.frr}}. Restricted to single checkpoints, it picked cycle 50 on MATR/CLO and cycle 300 on HUST in most splits and saved {{SEL.mc.single_sel.sav}}% and {{SEL.h.single_sel.sav}}%. The paired difference between the two was {{SEL.mc.seq_sel_vs_single_sel}} percentage points on MATR/CLO (95% CI {{SEL.mc.seq_sel_vs_single_sel_lo}} to {{SEL.mc.seq_sel_vs_single_sel_hi}}) and {{SEL.h.seq_sel_vs_single_sel}} points on HUST ({{SEL.h.seq_sel_vs_single_sel_lo}} to {{SEL.h.seq_sel_vs_single_sel_hi}}). **A single checkpoint chosen on training cells was therefore as efficient as the sequential rule on both data sets**; the advantage of the sequential rule over the single-checkpoint rule in Section 3.3 ({{SEL.h.dense_vs_single_100}} points on HUST) is an advantage over a checkpoint fixed at cycle 100, and a training-selected checkpoint removes it ({{SEL.h.single_sel_vs_single_100}} points gained over cycle 100). The value of trajectory calibration is thus not higher efficiency than a well-chosen single look, but valid error control when a laboratory looks at its cells repeatedly — which naive practice does, with the error inflation shown above — and the freedom to stop at any checkpoint without fixing one in advance.

### 3.6 Robustness and sensitivity

{{FIG:Fig8_robustness|Robustness of the screening rules (E4, {{CFG.n_secondary}} splits per configuration, elastic net). Larger of the mean false-pass and false-reject rates as a function of the calibration-set fraction, the training-set fraction and Gaussian feature noise (in multiples of each feature's training standard deviation) added either to calibration and test cells alike or to test cells only. Dashed line: nominal level 0.10. Cycling saved is given in {{STABREF:tables/T7_robustness.csv}}.}}

**Calibration and training set size.** The guarantee held for every calibration and training fraction tested ({{FIGREF:Fig8_robustness}}; {{STABREF:tables/T7_robustness.csv}}); what changed was efficiency. With only 10% of the MATR/CLO cells (about {{E4.mc.cal_fraction.0.1.proposed.ncal_bad}} bad and {{E4.mc.cal_fraction.0.1.proposed.ncal_good}} good cells) used for calibration, the rule saved {{E4.mc.cal_fraction.0.1.proposed.sav}}% of the cycling instead of {{E4.mc.cal_fraction.0.25.proposed.sav}}% with 25%, because with fewer than nine cells of a class the required order statistic does not exist and the rule cannot pass (or reject) at all in some splits. On HUST, a calibration fraction of 20% (about {{E4.h.cal_fraction.0.2.proposed.ncal_bad}} bad cells) reduced the saving to {{E4.h.cal_fraction.0.2.proposed.sav}}%. Smaller training sets lowered efficiency gradually (MATR/CLO {{E4.mc.train_fraction.0.2.proposed.sav}}% with 20% training cells versus {{E4.mc.train_fraction.0.5.proposed.sav}}% with 50%) without affecting validity. In practice, about 10–15 cells of each class are needed in the calibration set before the rule becomes efficient.

**Measurement noise.** When Gaussian noise was added to the features of calibration and test cells alike, the two remained exchangeable and the trajectory rule stayed valid at every noise level (false-pass rate {{E4.mc.noise_both.1.proposed.fpr}} and false-reject rate {{E4.mc.noise_both.1.proposed.frr}} on MATR/CLO with noise of one standard deviation), while its saving fell from {{E4.mc.noise_both.0.1.proposed.sav}}% to {{E4.mc.noise_both.1.proposed.sav}}%: noisier scores lead to wider thresholds and later decisions. The parametric rule, whose thresholds do not adapt to the data, became invalid ({{E4.mc.noise_both.1.gauss.fpr}} and {{E4.mc.noise_both.1.gauss.frr}}). Noise added to the test cells only — a model of screening cells on a noisier tester than the calibration cells — broke exchangeability, and from a quarter of a standard deviation upward every rule exceeded at least one target on MATR/CLO: the trajectory rule reached {{E4.mc.noise_test.0.25.proposed.fpr}} and {{E4.mc.noise_test.0.25.proposed.frr}} at a quarter of a standard deviation and {{E4.mc.noise_test.1.proposed.fpr}} and {{E4.mc.noise_test.1.proposed.frr}} at one standard deviation. The sequential rule was more sensitive to such a mismatch than the single-checkpoint rule, because every additional look gives the extra noise another chance to cross a threshold: on HUST, at a quarter of a standard deviation, the false-pass rate was {{E4.h.noise_test.0.25.proposed.fpr}} for the trajectory rule and {{E4.h.noise_test.0.25.single_k100.fpr}} for the single-checkpoint rule. Calibration cells must therefore be measured on the same equipment and protocol as the cells being screened, and the calibration should be repeated when the tester changes; Section 3.7 shows the same requirement, and the effect of recalibrating with a few target cells, for batch and laboratory shift.

**Specification and error levels (E7).** Moving $L^\ast$ changes the class balance ({{SFIGREF:Fig9_sensitivity_Lstar}}; {{STABREF:tables/T10_sensitivity.csv}}). On MATR/CLO the trajectory rule stayed valid for $L^\ast$ = 550, 750 and 900 cycles (cycling saved {{E7.mc.L550.proposed.sav}}%, {{E7.mc.L750.proposed.sav}}% and {{E7.mc.L900.proposed.sav}}%). On HUST, with $L^\ast$ = 1,600 only about {{E7.h.L1600.proposed.ncal_bad}} calibration cells were bad, too few to pass any cell at $\alpha$ = 0.10 (no pass in {{E7.h.L1600.proposed.never_pass}}% of the splits), and with $L^\ast$ = 2,200 only about {{E7.h.L2200.proposed.ncal_good}} were good; the rule remained valid but saved only {{E7.h.L1600.proposed.sav}}% and {{E7.h.L2200.proposed.sav}}%. The uncorrected Gaussian rule violated one of the targets at both alternative specifications on HUST (false-pass rate {{E7.h.L1600.gauss.fpr}} at 1,600; false-reject rate {{E7.h.L2200.gauss.frr}} at 2,200). Stricter error levels need more calibration cells: at $\alpha=\beta=0.05$ at least 19 cells per class are required, so the rule decided nothing early on HUST (about {{E7.h.ab_0.05_0.05.proposed.ncal_bad}} cells per class) and saved {{E7.mc.ab_0.05_0.05.proposed.sav}}% on MATR/CLO with error rates {{E7.mc.ab_0.05_0.05.proposed.fpr}} and {{E7.mc.ab_0.05_0.05.proposed.frr}}. At $\alpha=\beta=0.20$ it was valid on both data sets and saved {{E7.mc.ab_0.2_0.2.proposed.sav}}% and {{E7.h.ab_0.2_0.2.proposed.sav}}%.

### 3.7 Batch shift, laboratory shift and unseen protocols

{{FIG:Fig10_distribution_shift|Screening under distribution shift (E5, {{CFG.n_secondary}} splits per case, elastic net). Batch shift: models trained and calibrated on MATR batches b1 and b2 and applied to batch b3 or to the CLO batch b4, either with the source calibration cells or after recalibration with 20 or 30 cells of the target batch cycled to $L^\ast$ only. Laboratory shift: capacity-trajectory features only, MATR/CLO model or HUST model, calibrated on HUST cells and tested on other HUST cells ($L^\ast$ = {{D.h.Lstar}}). Points: mean error rates with 95% confidence intervals.}}

The guarantee rests on exchangeability between calibration and screened cells, and the shift experiments show what happens without it ({{FIGREF:Fig10_distribution_shift}}; {{STABREF:tables/T8_shift.csv}}). The MATR batches differ strongly: with $L^\ast$ = 750 cycles, all 43 cells of b2 but only 3 of the 40 cells of b3 and 18 of the 45 CLO cells are bad, because the batches were cycled with different protocol sets. A model trained and calibrated on b1 and b2 and applied to b3 without recalibration rejected about half of the good b3 cells (false-reject rate {{E5.b3_source.proposed.frr}}); its false-pass rate of {{E5.b3_source.proposed.fpr}} rests on the {{E5.b3_source.proposed.ntest_bad}} bad cells of b3 and is therefore imprecise. On the CLO batch b4 the false-reject rate was {{E5.b4_source.proposed.frr}}. The naive and Gaussian rules failed similarly, the single-checkpoint rule exchanged a low false-reject rate for a false-pass rate of {{E5.b4_source.single_k100.fpr}} on b4, and the point-prediction rule happened to stay within the targets on b3 but passed {{E5.b4_source.point_k100.fpr}} of the bad b4 cells — an uncalibrated rule can look safe on one batch and fail on the next.

Recalibration with target cells cycled only to $L^\ast$, keeping the source model, is informative only where the target calibration set contains enough cells of each class. With 30 CLO cells (about {{E5.b4_target30.proposed.ncal_bad}} bad and {{E5.b4_target30.proposed.ncal_good}} good; {{E5.b4_target30.proposed.ntest_bad}} bad and {{E5.b4_target30.proposed.ntest_good}} good test cells per split) the error rates were {{E5.b4_target30.proposed.fpr}} and {{E5.b4_target30.proposed.frr}} and {{E5.b4_target30.proposed.sav}}% of the cycling was saved; given the small test sets this is consistent with, but only weakly supports, restored validity. With 20 CLO cells (about {{E5.b4_target20.proposed.ncal_bad}} bad) and for b3 (about {{E5.b3_target20.proposed.ncal_bad}} bad cells among 20 or 30), fewer than the nine cells needed for a pass threshold were available, so the rule never passed a cell early ({{E5.b4_target20.proposed.never_pass}}% of the splits) and its zero false-pass rate holds by construction; it saved {{E5.b3_target20.proposed.sav}}–{{E5.b4_target20.proposed.sav}}% of the cycling. The Gaussian rule, recalibrated in the same way, kept a false-reject rate above 0.79.

Across laboratories, a model trained on MATR/CLO capacity trajectories and recalibrated on HUST cells was valid on the remaining HUST cells (false-pass {{E5.xlab_source.proposed.fpr}}, false-reject {{E5.xlab_source.proposed.frr}}), although it saved only {{E5.xlab_source.proposed.sav}}% of the cycling against {{E5.xlab_target.proposed.sav}}% for a model trained on HUST cells. Recalibration on the target population therefore transfers validity even when the base model comes from another laboratory; efficiency still depends on how well the model fits the new cells.

When whole charging protocols were held out, so that no protocol of a test cell was seen in training or calibration (E9, {{E9.n_groups}} protocol groups of MATR/CLO), the trajectory rule had false-pass and false-reject rates of {{E9.mc.proposed.fpr}} and {{E9.mc.proposed.frr}} and saved {{E9.mc.proposed.sav}}% of the cycling. This is a stress test outside the assumptions of Proposition 1, because cells sharing a protocol are not exchangeable with cells of other protocols; that the rates stayed near the targets suggests that, for protocols drawn at random from the same design space, protocol-level exchangeability is enough in practice. It does not cover protocols outside that space.

### 3.8 Calibration-set-conditional control and computation

**How often a single campaign misses the target.** Proposition 1 bounds error rates on average over calibration sets. For one particular calibration set the conditional false-pass probability can exceed $\alpha$, and with tens of calibration cells it does so often: in the main experiment the realised false-pass rate on the test cells exceeded 0.10 in {{E1.mc.proposed.fpr_above}}% (MATR/CLO) and {{E1.h.proposed.fpr_above}}% (HUST) of the splits, the false-reject rate in {{E1.mc.proposed.frr_above}}% and {{E1.h.proposed.frr_above}}%, and at least one of the two in {{E1.mc.proposed.either_above}}% and {{E1.h.proposed.either_above}}%. Part of this is test-set sampling noise (about {{E1.mc.proposed.ntest_bad}} bad and {{E1.mc.proposed.ntest_good}} good test cells per split on MATR/CLO), and part is the variability of the calibrated threshold itself: for 20 calibration cells of a class, the probability that the conditional error probability of a marginally calibrated threshold exceeds $\alpha=0.10$ is {{PAC.p_exceed_n20}}% ({{STABREF:tables/T13_conditional_error_analytic.csv}}). The single-checkpoint and lifetime-interval rules share this property; it is inherent to marginal conformal guarantees with small calibration sets.

**Training-conditional (PAC) calibration.** The PAC variant of Section 2.4 ($\delta=0.10$) targets this directly, at a large cost in efficiency ({{STABREF:tables/T12_pac.csv}}). On MATR/CLO, with about {{E10.mc.default.traj_pac.ncal_bad}} bad and {{E10.mc.default.traj_pac.ncal_good}} good calibration cells, it lowered the mean error rates to {{E10.mc.default.traj_pac.fpr}} and {{E10.mc.default.traj_pac.frr}} and saved {{E10.mc.default.traj_pac.sav}}% of the cycling instead of {{E10.mc.default.proposed.sav}}%; with about {{E10.mc.large_cal.traj_pac.ncal_bad}} bad and {{E10.mc.large_cal.traj_pac.ncal_good}} good calibration cells it saved {{E10.mc.large_cal.traj_pac.sav}}% (marginal rule {{E10.mc.large_cal.proposed.sav}}%). On HUST, with about {{E10.h.default.traj_pac.ncal_bad}} cells per class, no finite PAC threshold exists (at least 22 are needed at $\alpha=\delta=0.10$) and the rule never decided; with about {{E10.h.large_cal.traj_pac.ncal_bad}} per class it passed no cell in {{E10.h.large_cal.traj_pac.never_pass}}% of the splits and saved {{E10.h.large_cal.traj_pac.sav}}%. The share of splits in which the realised test-set error exceeded 0.10 fell to {{E10.mc.default.traj_pac.fpr_above}}% (false pass) and {{E10.mc.default.traj_pac.frr_above}}% (false reject) on MATR/CLO. This split-level share is only a proxy for the PAC property, because the realised rate on 20–40 test cells also varies with the test sample; it is consistent with, but does not verify, the $\delta=0.10$ bound. Calibration-set-conditional control is therefore practical only with several tens of calibration cells per class; with fewer, users should accept the marginal guarantee and plan for the per-campaign variability reported above.

**Computational cost.** The screening layer itself is negligible ({{STABREF:tables/T9_compute.csv}}): calibrating the two thresholds took {{E6.mc.calibration_only_n40.cal_ms}} ms for 40 calibration cells and {{E6.mc.calibration_only_n4000.cal_ms}} ms for 4,000, and deciding 1,000 cells over all checkpoints took about {{E6.mc.calibration_only_n4000.decide_ms}} ms on one CPU core. The cost of the procedure is that of training the per-checkpoint base predictors once per calibration campaign: on MATR/CLO, {{E6.mc.en.sec}} s per split for the elastic net (all {{D.mc.M}} checkpoints, including internal cross-validation), {{E6.mc.gp.sec}} s for the Gaussian process, {{E6.mc.cnn.sec}} s for the CNN, and {{E6.mc.rf.sec}} s for the random forest. Peak memory of a process running one split was about {{E6.mc.en.mem}} MB for every model, dominated by the imported numerical libraries. Even the slowest model needs well under a minute per calibration campaign, against the days to months of cycling that the screening saves, so computation is not a constraint in a test laboratory.

### 3.9 Physical basis of early decisions

{{FIG:Fig15_interpretation|Which early-cycle signals carry the screening decision (E11). Top: Spearman correlation between each feature and $\log_{10}$ cycle life over all cells alive at each checkpoint. Bottom: mean absolute standardised elastic-net coefficient of the four most used features over the training sets of {{CFG.n_secondary}} E1 splits; the dashed line sums all other features. Features are ordered by their largest coefficient.}}

The time at which a cell can be decided is set by when its early-cycle data begin to separate long- from short-lived cells ({{FIGREF:Fig15_interpretation}}). On MATR/CLO no feature correlated with cycle life by more than |ρ| = {{E11.mc.maxabs.k20}} at cycle 20. From cycle 40 onwards the variance of $\Delta Q_{k-10}(V)$ became the dominant signal (ρ = {{E11.mc.rho.dq_var.k40}} at cycle 40, {{E11.mc.rho.dq_var.k100}} at cycle 100), and from cycle 100 it carried the largest share of the elastic-net weight ({{E11.mc.share.dq_var.k100}}% at cycle 100). Severson et al. linked this change in the shape of the discharge voltage curve to degradation modes, such as loss of active material, that are not yet visible as capacity fade [@severson2019]. This timing matches the decision profile of the proposed rule: most MATR/CLO cells were decided between cycles 40 and 100 ({{FIGREF:Fig5_decision_time}}). Capacity-fade features (slope of the capacity trajectory, $Q_k-Q_{10}$) correlated with lifetime only after about 200 cycles, i.e. after most decisions had been taken.

The HUST cells behaved differently. Their early signal came mainly from the initial capacity at cycle 2 (ρ = {{E11.h.rho.q2.k20}}) and the intercept of the capacity trajectory (ρ = {{E11.h.rho.fade_intercept.k100}} at cycle 100), i.e. from cell-to-cell differences present at the start of life rather than from degradation. The statistics of $\Delta V(q)$, the HUST analogue of $\Delta Q(V)$, were only moderately informative (|ρ| ≤ {{E11.h.maxabs.k100}} for any feature at cycle 100), and the capacity-fade features became strongly informative only after about 600 cycles (ρ = {{E11.h.rho.fade_slope.k800}} for the fade slope at cycle 800). This is why the HUST cells were decided later and why less cycling was saved there than on MATR/CLO. A plausible reason is that every HUST cell was discharged with a different multi-step protocol, which changes the voltage-curve signatures that the MATR/CLO features exploit; we could not test this explanation directly. The comparison shows that the efficiency of any early-screening rule is limited by when the electrochemical signal appears, whereas the error guarantee does not depend on it.

### 3.10 Error analysis

{{FIG:Fig11_error_analysis|Error frequency of the proposed rule for each cell, across the splits in which the cell was a test cell (E1), against the distance of its cycle life from the specification, $\log_{10}(T/L^\ast)$. Bad cells (left) can only be falsely passed; good cells (right) can only be falsely rejected.}}

Errors concentrated near the specification ({{FIGREF:Fig11_error_analysis}}): the Spearman correlation between $|\log_{10}(T/L^\ast)|$ and a cell's error frequency was {{E8.mc.rho}} on MATR/CLO ($p$ = {{E8.mc.p}}) and {{E8.h.rho}} on HUST ($p$ = {{E8.h.p}}). Cells within 0.05 decades of $L^\ast$ (about ±12% in cycle life) had a mean error frequency of {{E8.mc.err_near}} on MATR/CLO compared with {{E8.mc.err_far}} for cells more than 0.1 decades away, and they accounted for {{E8.mc.share_err_near}}% of all errors. Errors far from $L^\ast$ came from a few cells whose early-cycle behaviour was not representative of their lifetime ({{STABREF:tables/E8_worst_cells_matr_clo.csv}}): the shortest-lived MATR cell (148 cycles) failed abruptly after early cycles that resembled those of ordinary cells and was falsely passed in most splits in which it was tested, and one long-lived cell (1,315 cycles) was falsely rejected in most splits because its early capacity-difference signature resembled that of short-lived cells. No early-cycle screening rule can classify such cells correctly from these features; the guarantee bounds how often such cells are mis-screened in aggregate but cannot identify them. On HUST the most frequent errors involved cells within about 6% of $L^\ast$ ({{STABREF:tables/E8_worst_cells_hust.csv}}).
