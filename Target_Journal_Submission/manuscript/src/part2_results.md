## 4 Results

### 4.1 Data and prediction accuracy

{{FIG:Fig2_lifetime_distributions|Cycle-life distributions of the two data sets and the specifications used in the main experiments. MATR/CLO: {{D.n_matr}} cells of Severson et al. and {{D.n_clo}} cells of the CLO validation batch of Attia et al.; HUST: {{D.n_hust}} cells of Ma et al. Dashed lines: $L^\ast$ = {{D.mc.Lstar}} and {{D.h.Lstar}} cycles; {{D.mc.bad_share}}% and {{D.h.bad_share}}% of the cells, respectively, are bad.}}

The specifications split both data sets roughly in half ({{FIGREF:Fig2_lifetime_distributions}}). The accuracy of the elastic-net predictor improved steadily with the number of observed cycles ({{FIGREF:Fig3_base_model_accuracy}}): on the test cells of all MATR/CLO splits, the mean absolute percentage error of the predicted cycle life fell from {{E1ACC.mc.k20}}% at 20 cycles to {{E1ACC.mc.k100}}% at 100 cycles and {{E1ACC.mc.k300}}% at 300 cycles; on HUST it was {{E1ACC.h.k20}}%, {{E1ACC.h.k100}}% and {{E1ACC.h.k1000}}% at 20, 100 and 1000 cycles. The error at 100 cycles on MATR/CLO is close to the 9.1% reported by Severson et al. for their full model on a fixed split [@severson2019], although our pooled data, random splits and feature set (no resistance features) differ from theirs. The score scale $s_k$ tracks the decrease of prediction error, which is what normalisation (Equation 2) exploits.

### 4.2 Validity of the screening decisions

{{FIG:Fig4_validity_and_efficiency|Validity (top) and efficiency (bottom) of the screening rules in experiment E1 ({{CFG.n_main}} random splits per data set, elastic net, $\alpha=\beta=0.10$). Top: mean false-pass rate among bad test cells and false-reject rate among good test cells with 95% confidence intervals; the dashed line marks the nominal level. Bottom: cycling time saved relative to cycling every test cell to $\min(T,L^\ast)$; rules whose error rate exceeds the nominal level are labelled.}}

{{TABLE:tables/T2_main_results.csv|Screening results of experiment E1 (elastic net, {{CFG.n_main}} random splits per data set, $\alpha=\beta=0.10$). FPR: false-pass rate among bad test cells; FRR: false-reject rate among good test cells; "upper" = upper limit of the 95% confidence interval of the mean over splits; early: share of test cells decided by the model before $\min(T,L^\ast)$; saved: fraction of cycling time saved relative to cycling every test cell to $\min(T,L^\ast)$.|dataset:Data set:s,method:Rule:s,fpr_mean:FPR:.3f,fpr_ci_hi:FPR upper:.3f,frr_mean:FRR:.3f,frr_ci_hi:FRR upper:.3f,accuracy_mean:Accuracy:.3f,early_rate_mean:Early:pct1,time_saving_mean:Saved:pct1,mean_time_mean:Cycles to decision:.0f}}

The trajectory-calibrated rule met both error targets on both data sets ({{FIGREF:Fig4_validity_and_efficiency}}, {{TABREF:tables/T2_main_results.csv}}). On MATR/CLO its mean false-pass rate was {{E1.mc.proposed.fpr}} (95% CI {{E1.mc.proposed.fpr_lo}}–{{E1.mc.proposed.fpr_hi}}) and its false-reject rate {{E1.mc.proposed.frr}} ({{E1.mc.proposed.frr_lo}}–{{E1.mc.proposed.frr_hi}}); on HUST they were {{E1.h.proposed.fpr}} ({{E1.h.proposed.fpr_lo}}–{{E1.h.proposed.fpr_hi}}) and {{E1.h.proposed.frr}} ({{E1.h.proposed.frr_lo}}–{{E1.h.proposed.frr_hi}}). The rates are below the nominal 0.10 because the maximum statistic is conservative when the scores of later checkpoints add little beyond earlier ones. As the theory predicts, the bound holds on average rather than in every split: the realised false-pass rate exceeded 0.10 in {{E1.mc.proposed.fpr_above}}% (MATR/CLO) and {{E1.h.proposed.fpr_above}}% (HUST) of the splits, with about {{E1.mc.proposed.ncal_bad}} and {{E1.h.proposed.ncal_bad}} bad cells, respectively, in the test sets' companion calibration sets. The single-checkpoint conformal rule, the proposed rule without normalisation and the lifetime-interval rule were also valid, as Proposition 1 (with $M=1$) and conformal theory imply.

The rules without a valid calibration behaved very differently. Using individually valid per-checkpoint thresholds sequentially (naive sequential) inflated the false-pass rate to {{E1.mc.naive.fpr}} on MATR/CLO and {{E1.h.naive.fpr}} on HUST, and the false-reject rate to {{E1.mc.naive.frr}} and {{E1.h.naive.frr}} — up to {{RATIO.naive_max}} times the nominal level. The Severson-style point rule at cycle 100 happened to stay below 0.10 on MATR/CLO ({{E1.mc.point_k100.fpr}} and {{E1.mc.point_k100.frr}}) but not on HUST ({{E1.h.point_k100.fpr}} and {{E1.h.point_k100.frr}}). The parametric rule with Gaussian thresholds on the same normalised scores was conservative on MATR/CLO ({{E1.mc.gauss.fpr}}, {{E1.mc.gauss.frr}}) and violated the false-reject target on HUST ({{E1.h.gauss.frr}}, upper confidence limit {{E1.h.gauss.frr_hi}}). Bonferroni calibration never passed or rejected a cell, because with about {{E1.mc.bonf.ncal_bad}} bad and {{E1.mc.bonf.ncal_good}} good calibration cells the required order statistic at level $\alpha/M$ does not exist.

### 4.3 Testing time saved

The proposed rule decides most cells within the first few checkpoints and sends the remainder to observation; the single-checkpoint rule decides at cycle 100; the naive sequential rule decides almost every cell early but violates the error targets; the dashed line is full observation to $\min(T,L^\ast)$.}}

On MATR/CLO the proposed rule decided {{E1.mc.proposed.early}}% of the test cells from their early cycles and saved {{E1.mc.proposed.sav}}% of the cycling needed to observe every cell to $\min(T,L^\ast)$ (mean {{E1.mc.proposed.time}} cycles per cell instead of {{D.mc.mean_full_cost}}); half of the cells were decided within {{E1.mc.proposed.time_median}} cycles and {{E1.mc.proposed.dec_by_100}}% by cycle 100 ({{FIGREF:Fig5_decision_time}}). On HUST, whose cells live longer and where the specification is 1,900 cycles, it decided {{E1.h.proposed.early}}% of the cells early and saved {{E1.h.proposed.sav}}% (mean {{E1.h.proposed.time}} instead of {{D.h.mean_full_cost}} cycles).

Among the valid rules, the proposed rule saved more cycling than the lifetime-interval rule on both data sets (by {{P.mc.interval_traj.diff}} and {{P.h.interval_traj.diff}} percentage points; Holm-adjusted $p$ = {{P.mc.interval_traj.padj}} and {{P.h.interval_traj.padj}}; {{TABREF:tables/T3_paired_efficiency.csv}}), and more than the unnormalised variant (by {{P.mc.traj_unnorm.diff}} and {{P.h.traj_unnorm.diff}} points). Against the single-checkpoint rule the result depended on the data set: on HUST the proposed rule saved {{P.h.single_k100.diff}} points more (95% CI {{P.h.single_k100.lo}} to {{P.h.single_k100.hi}}; $p$ = {{P.h.single_k100.padj}}; $r$ = {{P.h.single_k100.r}}), whereas on MATR/CLO the difference was {{P.mc.single_k100.diff}} points (95% CI {{P.mc.single_k100.lo}} to {{P.mc.single_k100.hi}}; $p$ = {{P.mc.single_k100.padj}}), i.e. not significant. The rules that saved more time than the proposed one — naive sequential, point prediction and, on HUST, the parametric rule — did so by violating at least one error target on at least one data set.

**Truncated calibration.** Because the proposed thresholds need only each calibration cell's class, the calibration campaign ended after at most $L^\ast$ cycles: a mean of {{C.mc.cal_wall_trunc}} wall-clock cycles per split on MATR/CLO versus {{C.mc.cal_wall_full}} for the full-lifetime calibration of the interval rule (factor {{C.mc.cal_wall_ratio}}), and {{C.h.cal_wall_trunc}} versus {{C.h.cal_wall_full}} on HUST (factor {{C.h.cal_wall_ratio}}). Total calibration cell-cycles fell by factors of {{C.mc.cal_cycles_ratio}} and {{C.h.cal_cycles_ratio}}.

### 4.4 Base predictors and checkpoint grids

The guarantee does not depend on the base predictor, and neither did validity in practice ({{STABREF:tables/T4_base_models.csv}}). With gradient boosting, random forests, Gaussian-process regression and the 1-D CNN, the proposed rule had false-pass rates of {{E2.mc.rf.proposed.fpr}}–{{E2.mc.gp.proposed.fpr}} and false-reject rates of {{E2.mc.gp.proposed.frr}}–{{E2.mc.rf.proposed.frr}} on MATR/CLO and saved {{E2.mc.gp.proposed.sav}}–{{E2.mc.rf.proposed.sav}}% of the cycling, close to the elastic net ({{E1.mc.proposed.sav}}%). On HUST the savings were {{E2.h.gp.proposed.sav}}–{{E2.h.cnn.proposed.sav}}% (elastic net {{E1.h.proposed.sav}}%). The only point estimate above a nominal level, the false-reject rate of the CNN on HUST ({{E2.h.cnn.proposed.frr}}), rests on 30 splits and its 95% confidence interval ({{E2.h.cnn.proposed.frr_lo}}–{{E2.h.cnn.proposed.frr_hi}}) includes 0.10. The parametric rule, in contrast, inherited the calibration of each model: it was conservative with the random forest and violated both targets with the Gaussian process on HUST ({{E2.h.gp.gauss.fpr}} and {{E2.h.gp.gauss.frr}}). A logistic classifier trained on cells truncated at $L^\ast$ — so that no training cell had to be cycled beyond the specification ({{STABREF:tables/T5_campaign_cost.csv}}) — remained valid ({{E2.mc.logit.proposed.fpr}}/{{E2.mc.logit.proposed.frr}} on MATR/CLO, {{E2.h.logit.proposed.fpr}}/{{E2.h.logit.proposed.frr}} on HUST) but saved less ({{E2.mc.logit.proposed.sav}}% and {{E2.h.logit.proposed.sav}}%), because a classifier trained on the binary label carries less information about how far a cell is from $L^\ast$.

{{FIG:Fig7_ablation_checkpoint_grid|Checkpoint-grid ablation (E3, {{CFG.n_secondary}} splits per grid). Bars: cycling time saved by the proposed rule, the proposed rule without score normalisation and the naive sequential rule; labels give the mean false-pass (FP) and false-reject (FR) rates. Single-checkpoint grids coincide for the three rules.}}

The choice of checkpoints mattered for efficiency, not for validity ({{FIGREF:Fig7_ablation_checkpoint_grid}}, {{STABREF:tables/T6_ablation_grid.csv}}). Every grid kept the proposed rule within the targets, whereas the naive rule's inflation grew with the number of looks (false-pass rate {{E3.mc.sparse_50_100_200_300.naive.fpr}} with four checkpoints and {{E3.mc.all16.naive.fpr}} with sixteen on MATR/CLO; {{E3.h.all17.naive.fpr}} with seventeen on HUST). Each additional look is paid for by a slightly higher threshold, so a sparse grid could beat the dense default: on MATR/CLO the grid 50/100/200/300 saved {{E3.mc.sparse_50_100_200_300.proposed.sav}}% against {{E3.mc.all16.proposed.sav}}% for all sixteen checkpoints. On HUST the best *single* checkpoint (cycle 300, {{E3.h.single_300.proposed.sav}}%) saved more than any sequential grid (dense grid {{E3.h.all17.proposed.sav}}%, sparse grid {{E3.h.sparse_100_300_600_1000.proposed.sav}}%), while a single checkpoint at cycle 100 or 1,000 saved only {{E3.h.single_100.proposed.sav}}% and {{E3.h.single_1000.proposed.sav}}%; on MATR/CLO the best single checkpoint was cycle 50 ({{E3.mc.single_50.proposed.sav}}%) and cycle 300 saved only {{E3.mc.single_300.proposed.sav}}%. A sequential grid therefore trades a few points of efficiency against the best fixed checkpoint for not having to know which checkpoint that is — knowledge that is not available before a campaign on a new cell type. Score normalisation helped mainly on dense grids, where unnormalised early scores dominated the maximum ({{E3.mc.all16.traj_unnorm.sav}}% versus {{E3.mc.all16.proposed.sav}}% saved on MATR/CLO).

### 4.5 Calibration-set size and training-conditional control

{{FIG:Fig8_robustness|Robustness (E4, {{CFG.n_secondary}} splits per setting): the larger of the mean false-pass and false-reject rates as a function of the calibration fraction, the training fraction, and Gaussian feature noise (in multiples of each feature's standard deviation) added to calibration and test cells or to test cells only. Dashed line: nominal level 0.10.}}

Smaller calibration sets kept the rule valid but made it more cautious ({{FIGREF:Fig8_robustness}}, {{STABREF:tables/T7_robustness.csv}}). With 10% of the MATR/CLO cells for calibration (about {{E4.mc.cal_fraction.0.1.proposed.ncal_bad}} bad cells per split) the rule saved {{E4.mc.cal_fraction.0.1.proposed.sav}}% instead of {{E4.mc.cal_fraction.0.25.proposed.sav}}% at 25%, and with 20% of the HUST cells (about {{E4.h.cal_fraction.0.2.proposed.ncal_bad}} bad cells) it saved {{E4.h.cal_fraction.0.2.proposed.sav}}% instead of {{E4.h.cal_fraction.0.3.proposed.sav}}% at 30%. With fewer than $\lceil 1/\alpha \rceil - 1 = 9$ calibration cells of a class no finite threshold exists and the rule cannot decide in that direction; this is the price of a finite-sample guarantee rather than a failure. Halving the training set cost less: {{E4.mc.train_fraction.0.2.proposed.sav}}% saved on MATR/CLO with 20% training cells and {{E4.h.train_fraction.0.2.proposed.sav}}% on HUST, with no loss of validity.

The guarantee is marginal over calibration sets. In {{E10.mc.default.proposed.fpr_above}}% (MATR/CLO) and {{E10.h.default.proposed.fpr_above}}% (HUST) of the splits the realised false-pass rate on the test cells exceeded 0.10, consistent with the analytic probability that the conditional error of a marginally calibrated threshold exceeds $\alpha$ ({{PAC.p_exceed_n20}}% for 20 calibration cells; {{STABREF:tables/T13_conditional_error_analytic.csv}}). The training-conditional (PAC) variant with $\delta=0.10$ (Section 3.3) reduced the share of splits above the target to {{E10.mc.default.traj_pac.fpr_above}}% (false pass) and {{E10.mc.default.traj_pac.frr_above}}% (false reject) on MATR/CLO, at a large cost in efficiency: {{E10.mc.default.traj_pac.sav}}% saved instead of {{E10.mc.default.proposed.sav}}%, rising to {{E10.mc.large_cal.traj_pac.sav}}% (versus {{E10.mc.large_cal.proposed.sav}}%) when about {{E10.mc.large_cal.traj_pac.ncal_bad}} bad and {{E10.mc.large_cal.traj_pac.ncal_good}} good cells were available for calibration ({{STABREF:tables/T12_pac.csv}}). On HUST, with about {{E10.h.default.traj_pac.ncal_bad}} cells per class, the PAC rule never reached a decision, because it needs at least 22 calibration cells of a class at $\alpha=\delta=0.10$; with about {{E10.h.large_cal.traj_pac.ncal_bad}} per class it still passed no cell in {{E10.h.large_cal.traj_pac.never_pass}}% of the splits and saved {{E10.h.large_cal.traj_pac.sav}}%. Training-conditional control is therefore practical only for calibration campaigns of several tens of cells per class.

### 4.6 Distribution shift and measurement noise

{{FIG:Fig10_distribution_shift|Batch and laboratory shift (E5, {{CFG.n_secondary}} splits per scenario). Mean false-pass and false-reject rates with 95% confidence intervals. b1+b2 → b3/b4: model and calibration from MATR batches 1–2, test cells from batch 3 or the CLO batch, calibrated on source cells (source_cal) or on $n$ cells of the target batch (target_cal). MATR → HUST: model trained on MATR/CLO or on HUST cells, calibrated on HUST cells.}}

The guarantee requires calibration and test cells to be exchangeable, and the shift experiments show what happens when they are not ({{FIGREF:Fig10_distribution_shift}}, {{STABREF:tables/T8_shift.csv}}). With model and calibration from MATR batches 1 and 2, every rule failed on later batches: the proposed rule had false-reject rates of {{E5.b3_source.proposed.frr}} (batch 3) and {{E5.b4_source.proposed.frr}} (CLO batch) and false-pass rates of {{E5.b3_source.proposed.fpr}} and {{E5.b4_source.proposed.fpr}}; the single-checkpoint rule falsely passed {{E5.b4_source.single_k100.fpr}} of the bad CLO cells. Recalibrating on target cells restored validity even with the source-batch model (false-pass {{E5.b4_target30.proposed.fpr}} and false-reject {{E5.b4_target30.proposed.frr}} on the CLO batch with 30 target cells), but with so few calibration cells the rule decided few cells early: it saved {{E5.b3_target20.proposed.sav}}–{{E5.b3_target30.proposed.sav}}% on batch 3, where 20 target cells contained only about {{E5.b3_target20.proposed.ncal_bad}} bad cells so that no cell could be passed, and {{E5.b4_target20.proposed.sav}}–{{E5.b4_target30.proposed.sav}}% on the CLO batch. Across laboratories the picture was the same: a MATR/CLO model calibrated on HUST cells was valid ({{E5.xlab_source.proposed.fpr}}/{{E5.xlab_source.proposed.frr}}) but saved {{E5.xlab_source.proposed.sav}}% of the cycling, against {{E5.xlab_target.proposed.sav}}% for a model trained on HUST cells. Recalibration buys back validity; efficiency under shift needs a model that transfers. When the cell population was varied only through the cycling protocol — training, calibration and test cells drawn from disjoint groups of the {{E9.n_groups}} MATR/CLO charging protocols (E9) — the rule remained valid ({{E9.mc.proposed.fpr}} and {{E9.mc.proposed.frr}}; upper confidence limits {{E9.mc.proposed.fpr_hi}} and {{E9.mc.proposed.frr_hi}}) and saved {{E9.mc.proposed.sav}}% ({{STABREF:tables/T11_protocol_grouped.csv}}), since calibration and test cells still came from the same protocol population.

Feature noise tests a subtler form of the same assumption ({{FIGREF:Fig8_robustness}}). When noise of equal size was added to the features of calibration and test cells, the proposed rule remained valid at all levels (largest error rate {{E4.mc.noise_both.1.proposed.fpr}} on MATR/CLO and {{E4.h.noise_both.1.proposed.frr}} on HUST at one standard deviation) and became more cautious ({{E4.mc.noise_both.1.proposed.sav}}% and {{E4.h.noise_both.1.proposed.sav}}% saved), whereas the parametric rule, whose Gaussian thresholds do not adapt to the noisier score distribution, exceeded both targets (up to {{E4.h.noise_both.1.gauss.frr}}). When noise was added to the test cells only — calibration on a cleaner measurement chain than the screening — the guarantee no longer applies, and the proposed rule degraded *faster* than the single-checkpoint rule: at 0.25 standard deviations its false-pass and false-reject rates were {{E4.mc.noise_test.0.25.proposed.fpr}} and {{E4.mc.noise_test.0.25.proposed.frr}} on MATR/CLO and {{E4.h.noise_test.0.25.proposed.fpr}} and {{E4.h.noise_test.0.25.proposed.frr}} on HUST, against {{E4.mc.noise_test.0.25.single_k100.fpr}}/{{E4.mc.noise_test.0.25.single_k100.frr}} and {{E4.h.noise_test.0.25.single_k100.fpr}}/{{E4.h.noise_test.0.25.single_k100.frr}} for the single-checkpoint rule. A rule that looks at a cell many times has many chances to cross a threshold calibrated on noise-free scores. Calibration cells must therefore be measured with the same equipment and feature-extraction chain as the cells to be screened.

### 4.7 Error levels, specification and computation

{{FIG:Fig9_sensitivity_Lstar|Sensitivity to the specification $L^\ast$ (E7, proposed rule, $\alpha=\beta=0.10$, {{CFG.n_secondary}} splits per setting): mean false-pass and false-reject rates and cycling time saved.}}

The rule met its targets at every combination of error levels and specifications tested ({{FIGREF:Fig9_sensitivity_Lstar}}, {{STABREF:tables/T10_sensitivity.csv}}). On MATR/CLO, tightening $\alpha=\beta$ from 0.10 to 0.05 reduced the error rates to {{E7.mc.ab_0.05_0.05.proposed.fpr}} and {{E7.mc.ab_0.05_0.05.proposed.frr}} and the time saved from {{E7.mc.ab_0.1_0.1.proposed.sav}}% to {{E7.mc.ab_0.05_0.05.proposed.sav}}%; relaxing them to 0.20 raised it to {{E7.mc.ab_0.2_0.2.proposed.sav}}%. Asymmetric levels ($\alpha=0.05$, $\beta=0.20$), appropriate when passing a bad cell is costlier than rejecting a good one, gave {{E7.mc.ab_0.05_0.2.proposed.sav}}%. On HUST, $\alpha=\beta=0.05$ requires at least 19 calibration cells per class; with about {{E7.h.ab_0.05_0.05.proposed.ncal_bad}} available the rule correctly abstained and saved nothing. Changing the specification moved the class balance: on MATR/CLO savings stayed between {{E7.mc.L900.proposed.sav}}% and {{E7.mc.L750.proposed.sav}}% for $L^\ast$ from 550 to 900 cycles, whereas on HUST $L^\ast$ = 1,600 and 2,200 cycles left only about {{E7.h.L1600.proposed.ncal_bad}} bad or {{E7.h.L2200.proposed.ncal_good}} good calibration cells per split, so that one decision direction was effectively unavailable and the saving fell to {{E7.h.L1600.proposed.sav}}% and {{E7.h.L2200.proposed.sav}}%. The size of the minority class in the calibration set, not the specification itself, limits efficiency.

The screening layer adds negligible computation ({{STABREF:tables/T9_compute.csv}}, {{SFIGREF:Fig14_compute}}). Calibrating the thresholds took {{E6.mc.calibration_only_n40.cal_ms}} ms for 40 calibration cells and {{E6.h.calibration_only_n4000.cal_ms}} ms for 4,000, and screening 1,000 cells at all checkpoints took about {{E6.mc.calibration_only_n400.decide_ms}} ms. Run time was dominated by fitting the base predictors: {{E6.mc.en.sec}} s per split for the elastic net and up to {{E6.mc.rf.sec}} s for the random forest on four CPU cores.

### 4.8 Error analysis

{{FIG:Fig11_error_analysis|Error frequency of the proposed rule for each cell, across the splits in which the cell was a test cell (E1), against the distance of its cycle life from the specification, $\log_{10}(T/L^\ast)$. Bad cells (left) can only be falsely passed; good cells (right) can only be falsely rejected.}}

Errors concentrated near the specification ({{FIGREF:Fig11_error_analysis}}): the Spearman correlation between a cell's distance from the specification (the absolute value of $\log_{10}(T/L^\ast)$) and its error frequency was {{E8.mc.rho}} on MATR/CLO ($p$ = {{E8.mc.p}}) and {{E8.h.rho}} on HUST ($p$ = {{E8.h.p}}). Cells within 0.05 decades of $L^\ast$ (about ±12% in cycle life) had a mean error frequency of {{E8.mc.err_near}} on MATR/CLO compared with {{E8.mc.err_far}} for cells more than 0.1 decades away, and they accounted for {{E8.mc.share_err_near}}% of all errors. Errors far from $L^\ast$ came from a few cells whose early-cycle behaviour was not representative of their lifetime ({{STABREF:tables/E8_worst_cells_matr_clo.csv}}): the shortest-lived MATR cell (148 cycles) failed abruptly after early cycles that resembled those of ordinary cells and was falsely passed in most splits in which it was tested, and one long-lived cell (1,315 cycles) was falsely rejected in most splits because its early capacity-difference signature resembled that of short-lived cells. No early-cycle screening rule can classify such cells correctly from these features; the guarantee bounds how often such cells are mis-screened in aggregate but cannot identify them. On HUST the most frequent errors involved cells within about 6% of $L^\ast$ ({{STABREF:tables/E8_worst_cells_hust.csv}}).
