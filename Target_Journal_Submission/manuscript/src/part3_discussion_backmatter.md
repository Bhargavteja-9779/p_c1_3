### 3.11 Implications for cycle-life testing

**Repeated looks must be paid for.** The most consequential result for practice is the behaviour of the naive sequential rule. Each of its per-checkpoint thresholds is a valid conformal threshold, and a practitioner who re-evaluates a prediction every few cycles and stops at the first confident call is implicitly using it. Its error rates were {{E1.mc.naive.fpr}} (false pass) and {{E1.mc.naive.frr}} (false reject) on MATR/CLO and {{E1.h.naive.fpr}} and {{E1.h.naive.frr}} on HUST at a nominal 0.10 — the optional-stopping inflation known from sequential analysis [@wald1945sprt; @howard2021cs] appears in battery screening at a practically relevant scale. Calibrating on the maximum over checkpoints removes this inflation at a cost that the data show to be modest, because scores of the same cell at successive checkpoints are strongly dependent; Bonferroni's union bound ignores that dependence and, with tens of calibration cells, reached decisions only on a two-checkpoint grid (Section 3.5).

**Truncated campaigns.** Because the class-conditional thresholds depend only on whether each calibration cell reached $L^\ast$, calibration cells can be retired at $\min(T,L^\ast)$ — the same observation that underlies conformal methods for censored lifetimes [@candes2023survival]. In our splits this shortened the calibration campaign (wall-clock time of the longest-running calibration cell) from {{C.mc.cal_wall_full}} to {{C.mc.cal_wall_trunc}} cycles on MATR/CLO (factor {{C.mc.cal_wall_ratio}}) and from {{C.h.cal_wall_full}} to {{C.h.cal_wall_trunc}} cycles on HUST (factor {{C.h.cal_wall_ratio}}); the total cell-cycles of the calibration campaign fell less, by factors of {{C.mc.cal_cycles_ratio}} and {{C.h.cal_cycles_ratio}}. The saving applies to the calibration campaign only: a regression base predictor still needs training cells cycled to end of life. A classifier of $Z$ trained on truncated cells removes that requirement as well, and kept the error guarantees but saved about ten percentage points less cycling (Section 3.4). The lifetime-interval rule, which needs calibration cells cycled to end of life, was less efficient, partly because it targets a stricter, two-sided goal: its realised class-conditional error rates were five to ten times below the target.

**One checkpoint or many?** With a checkpoint fixed in advance at cycle 100, the sequential rule saved as much cycling as the single-checkpoint rule on MATR/CLO and clearly more on HUST, whose informative checkpoints come later (Section 3.3). That advantage disappeared when the single checkpoint was chosen on the training cells (Section 3.5): a training-selected single checkpoint and a training-selected sequential grid saved the same cycling on both data sets. Trajectory calibration is therefore not a way to screen faster than a well-chosen single look. Its value is that it keeps the error rates valid when a laboratory looks at its cells repeatedly and stops at the first confident call — what the naive rule does, with error rates up to {{RATIO.naive_max}} times the target — and that it lets the laboratory stop at whichever checkpoint the data allow without committing to one in advance. In practice, a sparse grid chosen on training cells is a reasonable default.

**Model-based uncertainty.** A Gaussian threshold applied at every look to the same normalised scores was conservative on MATR/CLO but exceeded the false-reject target on HUST, and the point-prediction rule of Severson et al. had no control on HUST ({{E1.h.point_k100.fpr}} and {{E1.h.point_k100.frr}}). Part of the Gaussian rule's failure is the repeated-looks effect, because it applies an uncorrected threshold at every checkpoint, and part is that the score scale $s_k$ estimated on training residuals is optimistic; both are properties of how model-based uncertainty is typically used for early decisions. Correcting the Gaussian threshold for the number of looks (Bonferroni over the checkpoints, E13) made it valid on both data sets ({{E13.mc.gauss_bonf.fpr}}/{{E13.mc.gauss_bonf.frr}} and {{E13.h.gauss_bonf.fpr}}/{{E13.h.gauss_bonf.frr}}) but so conservative that it saved only {{E13.mc.gauss_bonf.sav}}% and {{E13.h.gauss_bonf.sav}}% of the cycling, against {{E13.mc.proposed.sav}}% and {{E13.h.proposed.sav}}% for the trajectory rule on the same splits. The same model, used with different decision thresholds, can be either wasteful or unsafe depending on the data set, whereas rank-based calibration adapts to the data at hand.

**What the guarantee does and does not mean.** Proposition 1 bounds error *rates* averaged over calibration and test draws from an exchangeable population. Three consequences matter for users. (i) For a given calibration set the conditional error can exceed the target: with 20 bad calibration cells, the probability that the conditional false-pass probability exceeds 0.10 is {{PAC.p_exceed_n20}}% ({{STABREF:tables/T13_conditional_error_analytic.csv}}); a training-conditional (PAC) variant removes this at the price of needing at least 22 calibration cells per class (Section 3.8). (ii) The guarantee is class-conditional, not cell-conditional: cells whose lifetime is close to $L^\ast$ carry most of the errors (Section 3.10), as expected for any screening rule. (iii) Exchangeability is an assumption about how the calibration cells were obtained; it is plausible for cells drawn from the same production lot and measured with the same equipment. In our experiments it failed when a later batch was screened with thresholds calibrated on earlier batches, and recalibration with target cells cycled only to $L^\ast$ was needed; a model trained in one laboratory and calibrated in the other stayed valid (Section 3.7). Extra noise on the screened cells alone also broke the guarantee (Section 3.6), so calibration and screening must share the measurement chain.

**Recommended workflow.** (1) Fix $L^\ast$, $\alpha$ and $\beta$ before testing, and choose a sparse checkpoint grid on training cells only. (2) Train a base predictor on any available historical cells (full-lifetime or truncated). (3) Cycle a calibration set of at least ten bad and ten good cells (preferably more than 22 of each for training-conditional control) from the population to be screened, to $\min(T,L^\ast)$ only. (4) Screen new cells sequentially with the trajectory-calibrated thresholds. (5) Recalibrate whenever the cell population, production batch or measurement equipment changes.

### 3.12 Limitations

* **One chemistry.** All {{D.n_total}} cells are LiFePO~4~/graphite 18650 cells of one type, from two laboratories. We tried to add cells of other chemistries, but the NCA/NCM data of Zhu et al. (Zenodo) and the NMC data of RWTH Aachen were not reachable from our computing environment (HTTP 403 and an interactive browser challenge), and the Sandia data available through BatteryArchive involve partial-depth cycling with many cells not reaching end of life. The guarantee itself does not depend on chemistry, but the efficiency results may.
* **Exchangeability and shift.** The guarantees require the calibration cells to be exchangeable with the cells being screened. Section 3.7 shows that they fail under batch shift without recalibration (laboratory shift was tested only with recalibration on the target laboratory), and Section 3.6 that extra measurement noise on the screened cells alone breaks them; the sequential rule was more sensitive to such noise than a single-checkpoint rule.
* **Marginal, class-conditional guarantee** (see Section 3.11); conditional error can exceed the target for a particular calibration set unless the PAC variant is used.
* **Retrospective evaluation.** Results come from repeated random splits of finite public data sets, not from a prospective screening campaign; the splits re-use the same cells, so split-to-split variation understates the variation across independent cell populations.
* **Cost model.** Time saved is measured in cell-cycles relative to cycling each cell to $\min(T,L^\ast)$; channel scheduling, rest periods and reference performance tests are not modelled.
* **Lifetime labels** are those of the data providers (first cycle at 80% of nominal capacity); other end-of-life definitions change the class labels [@li2026endpoint].

## 4 Conclusions

Early cycle-life predictions are routinely used to make go/no-go decisions, but the error rate of those decisions is rarely controlled, and the common practice of re-checking predictions as cycling proceeds silently inflates it. We showed that calibrating pass and reject thresholds on the maximum over checkpoints of normalised model scores gives finite-sample, distribution-free bounds on the false-pass and false-reject probabilities that hold under any stopping time, and that the calibration needs cells cycled only to the specification. On {{D.n_total}} commercial LiFePO~4~/graphite cells from two laboratories, the rule met its error targets with {{CFG.alpha}} nominal levels while cutting cycling by {{E1.mc.proposed.sav}}% (MATR/CLO) and {{E1.h.proposed.sav}}% (HUST) relative to cycling every cell to the specification, whereas naive sequential use of valid per-checkpoint thresholds exceeded the targets by up to a factor of {{RATIO.naive_max}}. A single checkpoint chosen on training cells was as efficient, so the gain from trajectory calibration is valid error control under repeated looks and flexible stopping, not faster screening; the guarantee holds on average over calibration campaigns and requires recalibration when the cell population changes. The procedure is a thin layer on top of any lifetime predictor, costs milliseconds to run, and every result is generated by a scripted pipeline whose code is available from the corresponding author on request.
