## Abstract

Early-cycle predictions of lithium-ion cell cycle life are increasingly used to stop long cycling tests early, but the error rate of the resulting pass/fail decisions is rarely controlled, and re-checking predictions as cycling proceeds inflates it. We present a distribution-free sequential screening procedure that, at a series of early checkpoints, passes a cell, rejects it or keeps it on test. Pass and reject thresholds are calibrated on the maximum over checkpoints of normalised model scores of held-out calibration cells. We prove that this bounds the probability of passing a cell that will not reach a cycle-life specification, and of rejecting one that will, at user-chosen levels under any data-dependent stopping time, and that calibration cells need to be cycled only to the specification rather than to end of life. On {{D.n_total}} commercial LiFePO~4~/graphite cells from two laboratories, with {{CFG.n_main}} random splits per data set and a nominal level of 0.10, the false-pass and false-reject rates were {{E1.mc.proposed.fpr}} and {{E1.mc.proposed.frr}} (MATR/CLO) and {{E1.h.proposed.fpr}} and {{E1.h.proposed.frr}} (HUST), while {{E1.mc.proposed.sav}}% and {{E1.h.proposed.sav}}% of the cycling needed to observe every cell to the specification was saved; calibration campaigns ended {{C.mc.cal_wall_ratio}} times sooner than with full-lifetime calibration. Sequential use of individually valid per-checkpoint thresholds exceeded the targets by up to {{RATIO.naive_max}} times, and parametric and point-prediction rules violated them on one data set. {{ABSTRACT_SHIFT}} The procedure works with any lifetime predictor and adds negligible computation.

**Keywords:** lithium-ion battery; cycle life; early prediction; battery testing; conformal prediction; sequential decision; uncertainty quantification

## 1 Introduction

Cycle-life testing is the slowest step in the development and qualification of lithium-ion cells. A cell that lasts {{D.mc.T_med}} cycles under fast charging occupies a cycler channel for weeks to months before its end of life (EOL, conventionally 80% of nominal capacity) is observed, and design or protocol comparisons need many such cells [@severson2019; @attia2020]. Data-driven early prediction has therefore attracted sustained attention. Severson et al. showed that features of the discharge-capacity curve difference between cycles 10 and 100 predict the cycle life of commercial LiFePO~4~/graphite cells with about 9% error [@severson2019]; Attia et al. embedded such predictions in closed-loop optimisation of fast-charging protocols and cut the experimental time from more than 500 to 16 days [@attia2020]; and hierarchical Bayesian models predicted protocol lifetimes from a few cycles [@jiang2021bayes]. Later work refined features and knee prediction [@attia2021stat; @fermin2020knee; @attia2022knees], used transfer or semi-supervised learning with unfinished cells [@ma2022hust; @guo2024semi; @zhai2025waste], combined physics with learning [@wang2024pinn], and built open benchmarks [@zhang2024batteryml]. Reviews in this journal and elsewhere identify uncertainty-aware lifetime prediction as an open need [@hu2020prognostics; @sulzer2021field; @bte2026piai].

In qualification and screening, however, the question asked of an early prediction is a decision: does this cell (or this design) meet a cycle-life specification $L^\ast$? Acting on a point prediction exposes the laboratory to an error rate that is unknown in advance. Ensemble, Gaussian-process or Bayesian uncertainty estimates [@tte2024ensemble; @tte2023knee; @jiang2021bayes] are model-dependent and are not guaranteed to be calibrated for a new set of cells. A second, less discussed problem is *repeated looking*. As cycling proceeds, engineers naturally re-evaluate the prediction and stop a test as soon as the prediction looks decisive. Even if each individual look controls its error, stopping at the first look that crosses a threshold inflates the overall error; this is the optional-stopping problem of sequential analysis [@wald1945sprt; @howard2021cs]. Finally, the uncertainty methods that do carry finite-sample guarantees, such as conformal prediction intervals for lifetime [@vovk2005alrw; @lei2018dfpi; @angelopoulos2023gentle], are calibrated with cells cycled to EOL, which brings back the long test campaign that early prediction was meant to avoid.

### 1.1 Prior work and research gap

**Early cycle-life prediction.** Following Severson et al. [@severson2019], early prediction has been extended with feature studies [@attia2021stat], knee-point prediction [@fermin2020knee; @attia2022knees], formation-stage diagnostics [@weng2021formation], deep transfer learning on cells with different discharge protocols [@ma2022hust], semi-supervised and incomplete-data learning [@guo2024semi; @zhai2025waste], physics-informed networks [@wang2024pinn], impedance-based features [@zhang2020eis] and benchmarks [@zhang2024batteryml; @dosreis2021data]. Papers in *Battery Energy* follow the same pattern of physically motivated features combined with machine learning and evaluated by point accuracy [@bte2024cnnlstm; @bte2025transfer; @bte2026lfp]. These works report prediction error at a fixed checkpoint; none provides a decision rule with a pre-specified, guaranteed error rate.

**Uncertainty for battery lifetime.** Ensembles, Gaussian processes and Bayesian hierarchical models provide predictive distributions [@jiang2021bayes; @tte2024ensemble; @tte2023knee], whose calibration depends on the model being correct; quantile regression forests give cycle-life ranges from early data [@zhang2023range]. Wu et al. [@wu2026cai] recently compared Bayesian, jackknife+, bootstrap and split-conformal intervals for early-cycle lifetime prediction on the MIT–Stanford cells; their intervals are computed at one fixed early checkpoint from cells cycled to end of life, and they are not used for stop/continue decisions. Conformal prediction gives finite-sample, distribution-free coverage under exchangeability [@vovk2005alrw; @lei2018dfpi; @romano2019cqr; @angelopoulos2023gentle] and has recently been applied to state-of-health forecasting [@arxiv2026conftl; @arxiv2026hwcp]. Evaluation practice for lifetime models under censoring and differing end-point definitions has also been examined [@li2026endpoint; @sulzer2021field].

**Statistical foundations.** Our thresholds are class-conditional order statistics, as in Neyman–Pearson classification [@tong2018np] and class-conditional set-valued classifiers [@sadinle2019lab]. Conformalised survival analysis uses lifetimes truncated at a cut-off to obtain valid lower bounds under censoring [@candes2023survival; @gui2024adaptive; @sesia2025dr]; conformal selection controls the false discovery rate when selecting units whose outcome exceeds a threshold [@jin2023selection]. Sequential validity over multiple looks is the subject of sequential testing and confidence sequences [@wald1945sprt; @howard2021cs] and, for early-exit neural networks, of anytime-valid nested prediction sets [@jazbec2024earlyexit]. Calibrating on the maximum of a score over an index set is also how simultaneous conformal bands for functional data are built [@lei2015functional; @diquigiovanni2022bands].

**Gap.** For early screening of battery cycle life, no existing method (i) controls per-cell false-pass and false-reject probabilities with finite-sample, distribution-free guarantees, (ii) remains valid when the test is stopped at a data-dependent time, and (iii) can be calibrated without cycling calibration cells to end of life. Our procedure adapts these order-statistic and maximum-score arguments to this setting; its novelty lies in the formulation and in the trajectory-wise calibration that makes the guarantee hold under sequential stopping with calibration data truncated at $L^\ast$, together with an extensive experimental characterisation on public battery data.

### 1.2 Aim and contributions of this work

This paper addresses these three problems for early cycle-life screening. Its contributions are:

1. **Formulation.** Early screening is posed as a sequential decision at checkpoints $k_1<\dots<k_M<L^\ast$ with three outcomes (pass, reject, continue cycling) and two class-conditional error constraints: a cell that will not reach $L^\ast$ ("bad") is passed with probability at most $\alpha$, and a cell that will reach $L^\ast$ ("good") is rejected with probability at most $\beta$.
2. **Trajectory calibration with a finite-sample guarantee.** Pass and reject thresholds are calibrated on the *maximum over checkpoints* of normalised model scores of held-out calibration cells. We prove that the two error constraints then hold for any data-dependent stopping time, without distributional assumptions beyond exchangeability of cells, and that each calibration cell needs to be cycled only to $\min(T,L^\ast)$ cycles, not to EOL. The guarantee holds for any base predictor, including one trained only on truncated data.
3. **Evaluation on two laboratories.** On {{D.n_total}} commercial LiFePO~4~/graphite cells from two laboratories ({{D.n_mc}} MATR/CLO cells [@severson2019; @attia2020] and {{D.n_hust}} HUST cells [@ma2022hust]) and 1,000 random splits per dataset, we compare the procedure with fixed-checkpoint, naive sequential, Bonferroni, parametric/Bayesian and lifetime-interval rules, five additional base predictors, ablations, robustness and sensitivity analyses, batch and laboratory shift, and an error analysis.
4. **Open, reproducible code** that regenerates every number, table and figure in this paper from the public raw data.

We stress what the paper does *not* claim: it does not propose a more accurate lifetime predictor, and its guarantees are statements about error *rates* over the population of cells exchangeable with the calibration cells, not about any individual cell.

## 2 Methods

### 2.1 Cells and cycling data

**MATR/CLO.** The 124 LiFePO~4~/graphite cells (A123 APR18650M1A, 1.1 Ah) of Severson et al. [@severson2019], cycled under 72 fast-charging policies with 4C discharge, and the 45 cells of the closed-loop-optimisation validation batch of Attia et al. [@attia2020], downloaded from data.matr.io. We applied the authors' cleaning steps and verified each against the data: five batch-1 cells that never reached EOL were removed; five batch-1 cells whose cycling continued in batch 2 were concatenated with their continuations (capacities match at the junction); six noisy batch-3 channels were removed. Cycle lives range from {{D.mc.T_min}} to {{D.mc.T_max}} cycles (median {{D.mc.T_med}}). **HUST.** The 77 cells of the same type tested by Ma et al. [@ma2022hust] with identical fast charging and 77 different multi-step discharge protocols, cycle lives {{D.h.T_min}}–{{D.h.T_max}} (median {{D.h.T_med}}). The HUST files are Python pickles; they were read with an allow-listed unpickler that permits only numpy/pandas reconstruction functions. Both datasets record data until the discharge capacity reaches 0.88 Ah (80% of nominal), and we use the providers' lifetime labels. The main specifications, $L^\ast=750$ (MATR/CLO) and $L^\ast=1900$ (HUST), are close to the median lifetimes, so that both classes are well represented; other values are studied in Section 4.5. Checkpoints are $\{20,30,\dots,100,120,140,160,180,200,250,300\}$ (MATR/CLO, $M=16$) and $\{20,30,\dots,100,150,200,300,400,500,600,800,1000\}$ (HUST, $M=17$). ### 2.2 Screening problem

Let cell $i$ have cycle life $T_i$ (number of cycles until the discharge capacity first reaches 80% of nominal) and let $L^\ast$ be the specification. The cell is *good* if $T_i \ge L^\ast$ ($Z_i=1$) and *bad* otherwise ($Z_i=0$). Measurements become available cycle by cycle. At checkpoints $k_1<\dots<k_M<L^\ast$ a cell that is still alive ($T_i>k_m$) yields a feature vector $X_i^{(m)}$ computed only from cycles $1,\dots,k_m$. A screening rule decides at each checkpoint to *pass* the cell (stop cycling, declare it good), *reject* it (stop, declare it bad) or *continue*. A cell that fails before a checkpoint is rejected by observation, and a cell still undecided after $k_M$ is cycled until $\min(T_i,L^\ast)$, which reveals its class without error. The targets are

$$\mathrm{P}(\text{rule passes cell } i \mid Z_i=0)\le\alpha,\qquad \mathrm{P}(\text{rule rejects cell } i \mid Z_i=1)\le\beta, \tag{1}$$

with as few cycles as possible spent before a decision.

### 2.3 Early-cycle features, base predictors and normalised scores

For each checkpoint a base predictor $h_m$ is trained on a training set of cells alive at $k_m$. In the main experiments $h_m$ is an elastic net [@zou2005enet] regressing $\log_{10}T$ on the checkpoint features, with penalty and mixing parameter selected by five-fold cross-validation inside the training set. The *normalised score* of cell $i$ at checkpoint $m$ is

$$S_i^{(m)}=\frac{h_m\!\left(X_i^{(m)}\right)-\log_{10}L^\ast}{s_m}, \tag{2}$$

where $s_m$ is the out-of-fold root-mean-square error of $h_m$ on the training cells (taken from the same cross-validation, so no additional fits are needed). Large positive $S$ is evidence that the cell is good. Normalisation puts checkpoints with very different prediction accuracy on a common scale. Other base predictors are used in Section 4.3: L2-regularised logistic regression on $Z$ (score = logit; trained only on $Z$, i.e. on cells cycled to $\min(T,L^\ast)$), gradient-boosted trees and random forests [@pedregosa2011sklearn] (scale = out-of-fold RMSE), a Gaussian process with automatic relevance determination [@rasmussen2006gpml] (scale = posterior standard deviation of each cell), and a one-dimensional convolutional network on the capacity-difference curve plus the scalar features (scale = RMSE on a 20% validation part of the training cells).

**Features.** For the MATR/CLO cells we generalise the features of Severson et al. [@severson2019] from cycle 100 to checkpoint $k$: the logarithms of the absolute minimum, variance, skewness and kurtosis of $\Delta Q_{k-10}(V)=Q_k(V)-Q_{10}(V)$; the discharge capacity at cycle 2; the maximum capacity minus that at cycle 2; slope and intercept of a linear fit to capacity over cycles $2,\dots,k$ and slope over the last 10 cycles; the capacity change between cycles 10 and $k$; the mean charge time of cycles 2–6; and the mean of the per-cycle maximum temperature. Internal-resistance features were not used because the CLO cells have no resistance records. During data auditing we found that some cycles are non-standard (rest or diagnostic steps), producing single-cycle glitches in $Q(V)$; $Q_k(V)$ and $Q_{10}(V)$ are therefore element-wise medians over cycles $k-4,\dots,k$ and $8,\dots,12$, which uses no cycle after $k$. The HUST cells were discharged with multi-step protocols, so $Q(V)$ is not single-valued; we use the analogous statistics of $\Delta V_{k-10}(q)=V_k(q)-V_{10}(q)$ on a grid of discharged capacity $q\in[0.02,0.85]$ Ah together with the same capacity-trajectory features. A unit test verifies that features at checkpoint $k$ are unchanged when all data after cycle $k$ are altered.

### 2.4 Trajectory calibration

A separate set of calibration cells, disjoint from the training cells, is scored with the trained $h_m$. Let $\mathcal B$ ($n_0$ cells) and $\mathcal G$ ($n_1$ cells) be the bad and good calibration cells. For every calibration cell define the trajectory statistics

$$P_i=\max_{m:\,T_i>k_m} S_i^{(m)}\quad(i\in\mathcal B),\qquad R_i=\max_{m} \left(-S_i^{(m)}\right)\quad(i\in\mathcal G), \tag{3}$$

(with $P_i=-\infty$ if a bad cell fails before $k_1$). The thresholds are the order statistics

$$\hat\lambda=P_{(r_0)},\qquad \hat\mu=R_{(r_1)}, \tag{4}$$

where $P_{(r)}$ is the $r$-th smallest value, $r_0$ is the smallest integer not less than $(n_0+1)(1-\alpha)$, $r_1$ is the smallest integer not less than $(n_1+1)(1-\beta)$, and an index larger than the sample size gives $+\infty$ (the rule then never passes, or never rejects). At checkpoint $m$ a cell still alive is passed if $S^{(m)}>\hat\lambda$, rejected if $S^{(m)}<-\hat\mu$ and otherwise kept on test.

**Proposition 1.** *Let the training cells be fixed, and suppose that the bad calibration cells and a new bad cell are exchangeable, and likewise the good calibration cells and a new good cell. Then for the new cell*
$$\mathrm{P}(\text{passed at some checkpoint}\mid Z=0)\le\alpha,\qquad \mathrm{P}(\text{rejected by the model at some checkpoint}\mid Z=1)\le\beta,$$
*for every rule that passes only when $S^{(m)}>\hat\lambda$ and rejects only when $S^{(m)}<-\hat\mu$, whatever its stopping time — in particular, whatever order the two checks are applied in, and whether or not an operator stops a test early without a decision.*

The proof (Supporting Information, Section S5) is short: a bad cell can be passed only if its trajectory maximum $P$ exceeds $\hat\lambda$, and by exchangeability the rank of $P$ among the calibration maxima is uniform, so this happens with probability at most $\alpha$ whatever the stopping time.

**Remarks.** (i) *Truncated calibration.* $P_i$, $R_i$ and $Z_i$ require only cycles up to $\min(T_i,L^\ast)$ because $k_M<L^\ast$; calibration cells never need to be cycled to EOL. If the base predictor is a classifier of $Z$, training cells also need only $\min(T_i,L^\ast)$ cycles. (ii) *Why the maximum.* Calibrating each checkpoint separately at level $\alpha$ ("naive sequential") controls each look but not the first crossing, whose error can approach $M\alpha$; Bonferroni calibration at $\alpha/M$ restores validity but, with tens of calibration cells, gives $\hat\lambda=+\infty$ whenever $(n_0+1)(1-\alpha/M)>n_0$, i.e. whenever $n_0<M/\alpha-1$ (fewer than 159 bad calibration cells for $M=16$ and $\alpha=0.1$). The maximum statistic accounts for the dependence between looks exactly. (iii) *Scope.* The guarantee is marginal over calibration and test cells and conditional on the class; realised error rates in any one batch of test cells fluctuate around it, and it says nothing about cells that are not exchangeable with the calibration cells (Section 4.6). Passing is possible only if $(n_0+1)(1-\alpha)\le n_0$, i.e. $n_0\ge(1-\alpha)/\alpha$ (at least 9 bad calibration cells for $\alpha=0.1$).

### 2.5 Comparison rules

All rules use the same base predictor, splits and cells. *Naive sequential*: per-checkpoint thresholds from the bad (good) calibration cells alive at that checkpoint at level $\alpha$ ($\beta$). *Bonferroni*: the same at $\alpha/M$ and $\beta/M$. *Single checkpoint*: class-conditional thresholds at cycle 100 only (valid, Proposition 1 with $M=1$). *Point prediction at cycle 100*: pass if the predicted lifetime is at least $L^\ast$, otherwise reject [@severson2019] (no error control). *Parametric threshold*: pass if $S^{(m)}>z_{1-\alpha}$ and reject if $S^{(m)}<-z_{1-\beta}$, i.e. a Gaussian predictive-bound rule; for the Gaussian process it is the Bayesian rule "posterior probability of being good above $1-\alpha$ / below $\beta$". *Lifetime interval*: trajectory-valid split-conformal lower and upper bounds on $\log_{10}T$ from normalised residuals [@lei2018dfpi], pass if the lower bound is at least $\log_{10}L^\ast$ and reject if the upper bound is below it; this rule needs calibration cells cycled to EOL and controls the error marginally rather than per class. *Proposed without normalisation*: Equations (3)–(4) applied to $h_m-\log_{10}L^\ast$.

### 2.6 Experimental protocol and statistics

The cell is the unit of analysis. In each random split, training cells are drawn within strata defined by dataset and class (50% of MATR/CLO cells, 40% of HUST cells), and the remaining cells are divided uniformly at random into calibration and test cells (MATR/CLO 25%/25%, HUST 30%/30%). Drawing calibration and test cells from the same pool makes them exchangeable, as Proposition 1 requires (an earlier version that stratified the calibration and test sets separately produced slightly different group mixtures in the two sets and an error rate marginally above the theoretical value; it was discarded). Every experiment uses split seeds fixed before running; the main experiment uses 1,000 splits per dataset, other experiments 200–333 (Table S1). Metrics are the false-pass rate among bad test cells (FPR), the false-reject rate among good test cells (FRR), accuracy, the share of cells decided by the model before $\min(T,L^\ast)$, the mean number of cycles to a decision, and the fraction of cycling time saved relative to cycling every test cell to $\min(T,L^\ast)$. Validity is assessed by the mean FPR and FRR over splits with normal-approximation 95% confidence intervals (Monte-Carlo error) and the share of splits exceeding the nominal level. For efficiency comparisons each cell's time to decision is averaged over the splits in which it was a test cell; methods are compared on these per-cell averages with paired Wilcoxon signed-rank tests [@wilcoxon1945], Holm correction over comparators, matched-pairs rank-biserial correlation as effect size, and a cell-level bootstrap [@efron1979bootstrap] (10,000 resamples) 95% CI for the difference in time saved. The experimental design, including all methods, metrics and tests, was fixed after a 100-split pilot (disjoint seeds) used only to detect data defects and estimate run time. Computations ran on four CPU cores with Python {{ENV.python}}, scikit-learn {{ENV.sklearn}} and PyTorch {{ENV.torch}}.
