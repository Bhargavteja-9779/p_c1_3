## Abstract

Early-cycle lifetime predictions of lithium-ion cells are increasingly used to stop cycling tests early, but the error rate of the resulting pass/fail decisions is rarely controlled, and re-checking predictions as cycling proceeds inflates it. We present a distribution-free sequential screening procedure that, at a series of early checkpoints, passes a cell, rejects it or keeps it on test. Pass and reject thresholds are calibrated on the maximum over checkpoints of normalised model scores of held-out calibration cells. We prove that this bounds the probability of passing a cell that will not reach a cycle-life specification, and of rejecting one that will, at user-chosen levels under any data-dependent stopping time, and that calibration cells need to be cycled only to the specification rather than to end of life. On {{D.n_total}} commercial LiFePO~4~/graphite cells from two laboratories, with {{CFG.n_main}} random splits per data set and a nominal level of 0.10, the false-pass and false-reject rates were {{E1.mc.proposed.fpr}} and {{E1.mc.proposed.frr}} (MATR/CLO) and {{E1.h.proposed.fpr}} and {{E1.h.proposed.frr}} (HUST), while {{E1.mc.proposed.sav}}% and {{E1.h.proposed.sav}}% of the cycling needed to observe every cell to the specification was saved; calibration campaigns ended {{C.mc.cal_wall_ratio}} times sooner than with full-lifetime calibration. Sequential use of individually valid per-checkpoint thresholds exceeded the targets by up to {{RATIO.naive_max}} times, and parametric and point-prediction rules violated them on one data set. Under batch and laboratory shift the guarantees failed unless thresholds were recalibrated on cells of the new population, restoring validity at reduced savings. The procedure works with any lifetime predictor and adds negligible computation.

**Keywords:** lithium-ion battery; cycle life; early prediction; battery testing; conformal prediction; sequential decision; uncertainty quantification

## 1 Introduction

Cycle-life testing is the slowest step in the development and qualification of lithium-ion cells. A cell that lasts {{D.mc.T_med}} cycles under fast charging occupies a cycler channel for weeks to months before its end of life (EOL, conventionally 80% of nominal capacity) is observed, and design or protocol comparisons need many such cells because cell-to-cell variation in ageing is large even within one production batch [@severson2019; @attia2020; @baumhofer2014production]. Data-driven early prediction has therefore attracted sustained attention. Severson et al. showed that features derived from the first 100 cycles, chiefly the variance of the discharge-capacity curve difference between cycles 10 and 100, predict the cycle life of commercial LiFePO~4~/graphite cells with about 9% test error for their full model [@severson2019]; Attia et al. embedded such predictions in closed-loop optimisation of fast-charging protocols and cut the experimental time from more than 500 to 16 days [@attia2020]; and hierarchical Bayesian models predicted protocol lifetimes from a few cycles [@jiang2021bayes]. Later work refined features and knee prediction [@attia2021stat; @fermin2020knee; @attia2022knees], used transfer or semi-supervised learning with unfinished cells [@ma2022hust; @guo2024semi; @zhai2025waste], combined physics with learning [@wang2024pinn], and built open benchmarks [@zhang2024batteryml]. Reviews in this journal and elsewhere identify uncertainty-aware lifetime prediction as an open need [@hu2020prognostics; @sulzer2021field; @bte2026piai].

In qualification and screening, however, the question asked of an early prediction is a decision: does this cell (or this design) meet a cycle-life specification $L^\ast$? Acting on a point prediction exposes the laboratory to an error rate that is unknown in advance. Ensemble, Gaussian-process or Bayesian uncertainty estimates [@tte2024ensemble; @tte2023knee; @jiang2021bayes; @richardson2017gaussian] are model-dependent and are not guaranteed to be calibrated for a new set of cells. A second, less discussed problem is *repeated looking*. As cycling proceeds, engineers naturally re-evaluate the prediction and stop a test as soon as the prediction looks decisive. Even if each individual look controls its error, stopping at the first look that crosses a threshold inflates the overall error; this is the optional-stopping problem of sequential analysis [@wald1945sprt; @howard2021cs]. Finally, the uncertainty methods that do carry finite-sample guarantees, such as conformal prediction intervals for lifetime [@vovk2005alrw; @lei2018dfpi; @angelopoulos2023gentle], have so far been calibrated in battery work with cells cycled to EOL [@wu2026cai], which brings back the long test campaign that early prediction was meant to avoid.

### 1.1 Prior work and research gap

**Early cycle-life prediction.** Following Severson et al. [@severson2019], early prediction has been extended with feature studies [@attia2021stat], knee-point prediction [@fermin2020knee; @attia2022knees], formation-stage diagnostics [@weng2021formation], deep transfer learning on cells with different discharge protocols [@ma2022hust], semi-supervised and incomplete-data learning [@guo2024semi; @zhai2025waste], physics-informed networks [@wang2024pinn], impedance-based features [@zhang2020eis], benchmarks [@zhang2024batteryml] and open data resources [@dosreis2021data]. Work in *Battery Energy* on state-of-health estimation and cycle-life prediction likewise combines physically motivated features with machine learning and evaluates point accuracy [@bte2024cnnlstm; @bte2025transfer; @bte2026lfp]. These works report prediction error at a fixed checkpoint; none provides a decision rule with a pre-specified, guaranteed error rate.

**Uncertainty for battery lifetime.** Ensembles, Gaussian processes and Bayesian hierarchical models provide predictive distributions [@jiang2021bayes; @tte2024ensemble; @tte2023knee; @richardson2017gaussian], whose calibration depends on the model being correct or on post-hoc recalibration without finite-sample guarantees; quantile regression forests give cycle-life ranges from early data [@zhang2023range]. Wu et al. [@wu2026cai] recently compared Bayesian, jackknife+, bootstrap and split-conformal intervals for early-cycle lifetime prediction on the MIT–Stanford cells; their intervals are computed at one fixed early checkpoint from cells cycled to end of life, and they are not used for stop/continue decisions. Conformal intervals have also been used for battery life prediction [@liu2026reliable] and to calibrate early-warning decision thresholds [@li2026early]. Conformal prediction gives finite-sample, distribution-free coverage under exchangeability [@vovk2005alrw; @lei2018dfpi; @romano2019cqr; @angelopoulos2023gentle] and has recently been applied to state-of-health forecasting [@arxiv2026conftl; @arxiv2026hwcp]. Evaluation practice for lifetime models under censoring and differing end-point definitions has also been examined [@li2026endpoint; @sulzer2021field].

**Statistical foundations.** Our thresholds are class-conditional order statistics, as in Neyman–Pearson classification [@tong2018np], class-conditional set-valued classifiers [@sadinle2019lab] and conformal outlier testing [@bates2023outliers]; calibrating decision thresholds with finite-sample error control is the subject of Learn-then-Test [@angelopoulos2025ltt]. Conformalised survival analysis uses lifetimes truncated at a cut-off to obtain valid lower bounds under censoring [@candes2023survival; @gui2024adaptive; @sesia2025dr]; conformal selection controls the false discovery rate when selecting units whose outcome exceeds a threshold [@jin2023selection]. Controlling error over repeated interim looks is the classical problem of group-sequential designs [@pocock1977group; @obrien1979multiple; @lan1983discrete; @jennison2000group], and validity at arbitrary stopping times that of sequential testing, confidence sequences and anytime-valid inference [@wald1945sprt; @howard2021cs; @ramdas2023game]. Conformal guarantees at data-dependent stopping times have been developed for early-exit neural networks [@jazbec2024earlyexit], early time-series classification [@ringel2024early] and spiking networks [@chen2025knowing]; concurrent work proposes any-stage-valid sequential conformal prediction with less conservatism than Bonferroni corrections [@li2026coins]. Calibrating on the maximum of a score over an index set is also how simultaneous conformal bands for functional data and trajectory-wide conformal regions are built [@lei2015functional; @diquigiovanni2022bands; @lindemann2023conformal; @cleaveland2024conformal], whereas multi-horizon conformal forecasting has used Bonferroni corrections over horizons [@stankeviciute2021conformal].

**Gap.** Each ingredient needed for early screening exists in the statistics literature: class-conditional conformal thresholds control false-pass and false-reject probabilities, calibrating on the maximum of a score over an index set keeps validity across many looks, and calibrating on a class rather than a lifetime allows censoring at a fixed cut-off. What is missing in battery testing is a screening rule that combines them — one that controls the class-conditional false-pass and false-reject probabilities with finite-sample, distribution-free guarantees when the test is stopped at a data-dependent checkpoint, using calibration cells cycled only to the specification — together with an honest assessment of when such a rule saves cycling and when it does not. The contribution of this paper is that combination and assessment, not a new statistical principle.

### 1.2 Aim and contributions of this work

This paper addresses these three problems for early cycle-life screening. Its contributions are:

1. **Formulation.** Early screening is posed as a sequential decision at checkpoints $k_1<\dots<k_M<L^\ast$ with three outcomes (pass, reject, continue cycling) and two class-conditional error constraints: a cell that will not reach $L^\ast$ ("bad") is passed with probability at most $\alpha$, and a cell that will reach $L^\ast$ ("good") is rejected with probability at most $\beta$.
2. **Trajectory calibration with a finite-sample guarantee.** Pass and reject thresholds are calibrated on the *maximum over checkpoints* of normalised model scores of held-out calibration cells. The two error constraints then hold on average over calibration sets for any data-dependent stopping time, without distributional assumptions beyond exchangeability of cells, and each calibration cell needs to be cycled only to $\min(T,L^\ast)$ cycles, not to EOL. The guarantee holds for any base predictor, including one trained only on truncated data; a training-conditional variant is also given.
3. **Evaluation on two laboratories.** On {{D.n_total}} commercial LiFePO~4~/graphite cells from two laboratories ({{D.n_mc}} MATR/CLO cells [@severson2019; @attia2020] and {{D.n_hust}} HUST cells [@ma2022hust]) and 1,000 random splits per dataset, we compare the procedure with single-checkpoint rules (fixed, or chosen on training cells), naive sequential, Bonferroni, Gaussian/Bayesian (with and without correction for repeated looks), point-prediction and lifetime-interval rules, and report five additional base predictors, checkpoint-grid ablations, robustness and sensitivity analyses, batch and laboratory transfer, and an error analysis, including the conditions under which the sequential rule does not help.
4. **Open, reproducible code** that regenerates every number, table and figure in this paper from the public raw data.

We stress what the paper does *not* claim: it does not propose a more accurate lifetime predictor, and its guarantees are statements about error *rates* over the population of cells exchangeable with the calibration cells, not about any individual cell.

## 2 Methods

### 2.1 Cells and cycling data

**MATR/CLO.** The 124 LiFePO~4~/graphite cells (A123 APR18650M1A, 1.1 Ah) of Severson et al. [@severson2019], cycled under 72 fast-charging policies with 4C discharge, and the 45 cells of the closed-loop-optimisation validation batch of Attia et al. [@attia2020], downloaded from data.matr.io. We applied the authors' cleaning steps and verified each against the data: five batch-1 cells that never reached EOL were removed; five batch-1 cells whose cycling continued in batch 2 were concatenated with their continuations (capacities match at the junction); six noisy batch-3 channels were removed. Cycle lives range from {{D.mc.T_min}} to {{D.mc.T_max}} cycles (median {{D.mc.T_med}}). **HUST.** The 77 cells of the same type tested by Ma et al. [@ma2022hust] with identical fast charging and 77 different multi-step discharge protocols, cycle lives {{D.h.T_min}}–{{D.h.T_max}} (median {{D.h.T_med}}). The HUST files are Python pickles; they were read with an allow-listed unpickler that permits only numpy/pandas reconstruction functions. In both datasets the lifetime label is the cycle at which the discharge capacity first falls to 0.88 Ah (80% of the 1.1 Ah nominal capacity); we use the providers' labels. Because initial capacities and discharge protocols differ between the data sets (4C constant-current discharge for MATR/CLO, multi-step discharge for HUST), this fixed-capacity end point corresponds to different fractions of each cell's initial capacity and is not physically identical across the two data sets; the screening problem is therefore defined separately for each data set. The main specifications, $L^\ast=750$ (MATR/CLO) and $L^\ast=1900$ (HUST), are close to the median lifetimes, so that both classes are well represented; other values are studied in Section 3.6. Checkpoints are $\{20,30,\dots,100,120,140,160,180,200,250,300\}$ (MATR/CLO, $M=16$) and $\{20,30,\dots,100,150,200,300,400,500,600,800,1000\}$ (HUST, $M=17$). ### 2.2 Screening problem

Let cell $i$ have cycle life $T_i$ (number of cycles until the discharge capacity first reaches 80% of nominal) and let $L^\ast$ be the specification. The cell is *good* if $T_i \ge L^\ast$ ($Z_i=1$) and *bad* otherwise ($Z_i=0$). Measurements become available cycle by cycle. At checkpoints $k_1<\dots<k_M<L^\ast$ a cell that is still alive ($T_i>k_m$) yields a feature vector $X_i^{(m)}$ computed only from cycles $1,\dots,k_m$. A screening rule decides at each checkpoint to *pass* the cell (stop cycling, declare it good), *reject* it (stop, declare it bad) or *continue*. A cell that fails before a checkpoint is rejected by observation, and a cell still undecided after $k_M$ is cycled until $\min(T_i,L^\ast)$, which reveals its class without error. The targets are

$$\mathrm{P}(\text{rule passes cell } i \mid Z_i=0)\le\alpha,\qquad \mathrm{P}(\text{rule rejects cell } i \mid Z_i=1)\le\beta, \tag{1}$$

with as few cycles as possible spent before a decision. The decision unit is the individual cell. This matches situations in which cells of the same type are cycled side by side and each test can be stopped separately — pruning a test matrix of charging protocols or operating conditions, or grading cells for a downstream use — but not lot-level qualification, where a decision about a whole production lot is required and which calls for different statistics.

### 2.3 Early-cycle features, base predictors and normalised scores

For each checkpoint a base predictor $h_m$ is trained on a training set of cells alive at $k_m$. In the main experiments $h_m$ is an elastic net [@zou2005enet] regressing $\log_{10}T$ on the checkpoint features, with penalty and mixing parameter selected by five-fold cross-validation inside the training set. The *normalised score* of cell $i$ at checkpoint $m$ is

$$S_i^{(m)}=\frac{h_m\!\left(X_i^{(m)}\right)-\log_{10}L^\ast}{s_m}, \tag{2}$$

where $s_m$ is the out-of-fold root-mean-square error of $h_m$ on the training cells (taken from the same cross-validation, so no additional fits are needed). Because $s_m$ is the minimum cross-validated error over the penalty grid, it is slightly optimistic: in the main experiment the root-mean-square error on test cells exceeded $s_m$ by 2–26% depending on the checkpoint. The conformal rules below calibrate on the scores themselves and do not rely on $s_m$ being an accurate error estimate; the Gaussian comparator does. Large positive $S$ is evidence that the cell is good. Normalisation puts checkpoints with very different prediction accuracy on a common scale. Other base predictors are used in Section 3.4: L2-regularised logistic regression on $Z$ (score = logit; trained only on $Z$, i.e. on cells cycled to $\min(T,L^\ast)$), gradient-boosted trees and random forests [@pedregosa2011sklearn] (scale = out-of-fold RMSE), a Gaussian process with automatic relevance determination [@rasmussen2006gpml] (scale = posterior standard deviation of each cell), and a one-dimensional convolutional network on the capacity-difference curve plus the scalar features (scale = RMSE on a 20% validation part of the training cells).

**Features.** For the MATR/CLO cells we generalise the features of Severson et al. [@severson2019] from cycle 100 to checkpoint $k$: the logarithms of the absolute minimum, variance, skewness and kurtosis of $\Delta Q_{k-10}(V)=Q_k(V)-Q_{10}(V)$; the discharge capacity at cycle 2; the maximum capacity minus that at cycle 2; slope and intercept of a linear fit to capacity over cycles $2,\dots,k$ and slope over the last 10 cycles; the capacity change between cycles 10 and $k$; the mean charge time of cycles 2–6; and the mean of the per-cycle maximum temperature. Internal-resistance features were not used because the CLO cells have no resistance records. During data auditing we found that some cycles are non-standard (rest or diagnostic steps), producing single-cycle glitches in $Q(V)$; $Q_k(V)$ and $Q_{10}(V)$ are therefore element-wise medians over cycles $k-4,\dots,k$ and $8,\dots,12$, which uses no cycle after $k$. The HUST cells were discharged with multi-step protocols, so $Q(V)$ is not single-valued; we use the analogous statistics of $\Delta V_{k-10}(q)=V_k(q)-V_{10}(q)$ on a grid of discharged capacity $q\in[0.02,0.85]$ Ah together with the same capacity-trajectory features. A unit test verifies that features at checkpoint $k$ are unchanged when all data after cycle $k$ are altered.

### 2.4 Trajectory calibration

A separate set of calibration cells, disjoint from the training cells, is scored with the trained $h_m$. Let $\mathcal B$ ($n_0$ cells) and $\mathcal G$ ($n_1$ cells) be the bad and good calibration cells. For every calibration cell define the trajectory statistics

$$P_i=\max_{m:\,T_i>k_m} S_i^{(m)}\quad(i\in\mathcal B),\qquad R_i=\max_{m} \left(-S_i^{(m)}\right)\quad(i\in\mathcal G), \tag{3}$$

(with $P_i=-\infty$ if a bad cell fails before $k_1$). The thresholds are the order statistics

$$\hat\lambda=P_{(r_0)},\qquad \hat\mu=R_{(r_1)}, \tag{4}$$

where $P_{(r)}$ is the $r$-th smallest value, $r_0$ is the smallest integer not less than $(n_0+1)(1-\alpha)$, $r_1$ is the smallest integer not less than $(n_1+1)(1-\beta)$, and an index larger than the sample size gives $+\infty$ (the rule then never passes, or never rejects). At checkpoint $m$ a cell still alive is passed if $S^{(m)}>\hat\lambda$, rejected if $S^{(m)}<-\hat\mu$ and otherwise kept on test.

**Proposition 1.** *Let the training cells be fixed, and suppose that the bad calibration cells and a new bad cell are exchangeable, and likewise the good calibration cells and a new good cell. Then for the new cell*
$$\mathrm{P}(\text{passed at some checkpoint}\mid Z=0)\le\alpha,\qquad \mathrm{P}(\text{rejected by the model at some checkpoint}\mid Z=1)\le\beta,$$
*for every rule that passes only when $S^{(m)}>\hat\lambda$ and rejects only when $S^{(m)}<-\hat\mu$, whatever its stopping time — in particular, whatever order the two checks are applied in, and whether or not an operator stops a test early without a decision.*

The proof (Supporting Information, Section S5) is short: a bad cell can be passed only if its trajectory maximum $P$ exceeds $\hat\lambda$, and by exchangeability the rank of $P$ among the calibration maxima is uniform, so this happens with probability at most $\alpha$ whatever the stopping time.

**Remarks.** (i) *Truncated calibration.* $P_i$, $R_i$ and $Z_i$ require only cycles up to $\min(T_i,L^\ast)$ because $k_M<L^\ast$; calibration cells never need to be cycled to EOL. This follows from calibrating on the class $Z$ rather than on the lifetime and is the deterministic (administrative) special case of the censoring device of conformalised survival analysis [@candes2023survival], with the cut-off at $L^\ast$; no censoring weights are needed. It is shared by any class-conditional conformal rule, including the single-checkpoint rule below. The regression base predictor is still trained on cells with known lifetimes; if the base predictor is a classifier of $Z$, training cells also need only $\min(T_i,L^\ast)$ cycles. (ii) *Why the maximum.* Calibrating each checkpoint separately at level $\alpha$ ("naive sequential") controls each look but not the first crossing, whose error can approach $M\alpha$; Bonferroni calibration at $\alpha/M$ restores validity but, with tens of calibration cells, gives $\hat\lambda=+\infty$ whenever $(n_0+1)(1-\alpha/M)>n_0$, i.e. whenever $n_0<M/\alpha-1$ (fewer than 159 bad calibration cells for $M=16$ and $\alpha=0.1$). The maximum statistic accounts for the dependence between looks exactly; it is the conformal counterpart of the max-type scores used for simultaneous validity over trajectories [@lindemann2023conformal; @cleaveland2024conformal] and of the boundaries of group-sequential designs [@jennison2000group]. (iii) *Scope.* The guarantee is marginal over calibration and test cells and conditional on the class; realised error rates in any one batch of test cells fluctuate around it, and it says nothing about cells that are not exchangeable with the calibration cells (Section 3.7). Passing is possible only if $(n_0+1)(1-\alpha)\le n_0$, i.e. $n_0\ge(1-\alpha)/\alpha$ (at least 9 bad calibration cells for $\alpha=0.1$, 19 for $\alpha=0.05$). Because the guarantee averages over calibration sets, the error probability *conditional on a particular calibration set* exceeds $\alpha$ with appreciable probability ({{STABREF:tables/T13_conditional_error_analytic.csv}}); realised error rates in individual splits are reported alongside the means. (iv) *Training-conditional (PAC) variant.* Replacing $r_0$ by the smallest $r$ with $F_{\mathrm{Beta}}(1-\alpha;\,r,\,n_0+1-r)\le\delta$ (and likewise $r_1$) gives thresholds for which the conditional false-pass probability exceeds $\alpha$ with probability at most $\delta$ over the draw of calibration cells [@vovk2005alrw; @angelopoulos2023gentle]. This requires i.i.d. calibration cells and continuous scores, and a finite rank exists only for $n_0\ge22$ at $\alpha=\delta=0.10$. We use $\delta=0.10$.

### 2.5 Comparison rules

All rules use the same base predictor, splits and cells. *Naive sequential*: per-checkpoint thresholds from the bad (good) calibration cells alive at that checkpoint at level $\alpha$ ($\beta$). *Bonferroni*: the same at $\alpha/M$ and $\beta/M$. *Single checkpoint*: class-conditional thresholds at cycle 100 only (valid, Proposition 1 with $M=1$); in Section 3.5 the single checkpoint is also chosen per split from the training cells only, which keeps the guarantee. *Point prediction at cycle 100*: pass if the predicted lifetime is at least $L^\ast$, otherwise reject [@severson2019] (no error control). *Gaussian threshold at every look*: pass if $S^{(m)}>z_{1-\alpha}$ and reject if $S^{(m)}<-z_{1-\beta}$ at every checkpoint, i.e. a Gaussian predictive-bound rule without correction for repeated looks; for the Gaussian process it is the Bayesian rule "posterior probability of being good above $1-\alpha$ / below $\beta$". A multiplicity-corrected version uses $z_{1-\alpha/M}$ and $z_{1-\beta/M}$ (Section 3.6). *Lifetime interval*: trajectory-valid split-conformal lower and upper bounds on $\log_{10}T$ from normalised residuals [@lei2018dfpi], pass if the lower bound is at least $\log_{10}L^\ast$ and reject if the upper bound is below it; this rule needs calibration cells cycled to EOL and controls the miscoverage of the lifetime bound over all cells rather than the class-conditional error, so it spends far less than the nominal error budget and serves as a reference for lifetime-interval practice rather than as a like-for-like competitor. *Proposed without normalisation*: Equations (3)–(4) applied to $h_m-\log_{10}L^\ast$.

### 2.6 Experimental protocol and statistics

The cell is the unit of analysis. In each random split, training cells are drawn within strata defined by dataset and class (50% of MATR/CLO cells, 40% of HUST cells), and the remaining cells are divided uniformly at random into calibration and test cells (MATR/CLO 25%/25%, HUST 30%/30%). Drawing calibration and test cells from the same pool makes them exchangeable, as Proposition 1 requires (an earlier version that stratified the calibration and test sets separately produced slightly different group mixtures in the two sets and an error rate marginally above the theoretical value; it was discarded). Every experiment uses split seeds fixed before running; the main experiment (E1) uses 1,000 splits per dataset, the other experiments 200 splits per configuration (CNN: 30), with designs listed in Table S1: E2 base predictors; E3 checkpoint grids; E4 calibration and training set size and feature noise; E5 batch shift (model and calibration from MATR batches 1–2, test on batch 3 or CLO, with or without recalibration on target cells) and laboratory transfer (MATR/CLO model, HUST calibration); E6 computation; E7 error levels and specification; E9 protocol-disjoint splits; E10 PAC calibration; E11 physical interpretation of early decisions; E12 checkpoint grid chosen on training cells; E13 multiplicity-corrected Gaussian rule, Bonferroni on sparse grids and persistent per-cell noise. E9–E13 were added after the design was fixed (E12 and E13 in response to internal review) and are reported as such. Metrics are the false-pass rate among bad test cells (FPR), the false-reject rate among good test cells (FRR), accuracy, the share of cells decided by the model before $\min(T,L^\ast)$, the mean number of cycles to a decision, and the fraction of cycling time saved relative to cycling every test cell to $\min(T,L^\ast)$. Validity is assessed by the mean FPR and FRR over splits with normal-approximation 95% confidence intervals and the share of splits in which the realised FPR, the realised FRR, or either, exceeded the nominal level. Because the splits re-use the same finite set of cells, the split-level confidence intervals describe Monte-Carlo error and understate the variation across independent cell populations. For efficiency comparisons each cell's time to decision is averaged over the splits in which it was a test cell; methods are compared on these per-cell averages with paired Wilcoxon signed-rank tests [@wilcoxon1945], Holm correction over comparators [@holm1979], matched-pairs rank-biserial correlation as effect size, and a bootstrap [@efron1979bootstrap] (10,000 resamples) 95% CI for the difference in time saved, resampling cells on HUST (each cell has its own discharge protocol) and charging-protocol groups on MATR/CLO, whose cells share protocols. The experimental design, including all methods, metrics and tests, was fixed after a 100-split pilot (disjoint seeds) used only to detect data defects and estimate run time. Computations ran on four CPU cores with Python {{ENV.python}}, scikit-learn {{ENV.sklearn}} and PyTorch {{ENV.torch}}.
